All tiers · 2002-03-30 · By Nate Fischer, Ball Theory

What Is an Athletic?

The A's own three baseball teams, a rulebook nobody has finished writing, and a theory of the offseason that ends at 11:59 tonight.

Here’s a question I haven’t been able to stop thinking about for three weeks: how many baseball teams do the Oakland A’s own?

The obvious answer is three. There’s the Oakland Athletics, in MLB. There’s the New Orleans Athletics, in the ABL. And there’s the Alaska Athletics, in the FBL, who play in a ballpark called The Wave, which I have to assume is a joke nobody in Alaska finds funny.

But I’m not sure the obvious answer is right. Three teams that share a name, an owner, a general manager’s brain and, for half of each year, a roster isn’t really three teams. It isn’t one team either. It’s something else, a sort of team-shaped cloud that condenses into three distinct shapes for six months and then evaporates back into a single pool every October. As of tomorrow, the cloud condenses. Today, it’s still a cloud, and the A’s are spending every remaining minute of the cloud using it.

The rules, as best I can reconstruct them

I’ve read the relevant section of the new league’s founding agreement maybe 20 times. Here’s what it says, roughly:

  1. The three Athletics clubs may never have more than one team in the top division at a time.
  2. New Orleans and Alaska can’t be promoted. If either one finishes in a promotion spot, the spot is “voided.”
  3. The three clubs may exchange players “freely,” but only during the offseason.
  4. Alaska carries “a heavy amateur draft penalty,” the specifics of which, according to the document itself, are still to be determined.

I want to linger on No. 4 for a second, because I’ve read a lot of rulebooks and I’ve never seen one that includes a rule that’s just a shrug. The penalty is heavy. We know that. We don’t know what it is. Nobody knows what it is. It’s the dark matter of the league’s governing documents: you can see its effects on everything around it, and you can’t observe it directly.

A league spokesman told me the penalty “will be finalized in due course.” I asked when due course was. He said, “In due course.”

Edge cases, in descending order of how much I want them to happen

New Orleans finishes in a promotion spot. This is the obvious one, and the most interesting, because nobody can tell me what “voided” means. If the A’s in New Orleans win the ABL’s American Championship, does the team they beat get promoted anyway? Does the semifinal loser get bumped up? Does the spot just disappear, so that only one team goes up?

And if only one team goes up, does that mean only one FBL team comes down to replace it? Or only two ABL teams get relegated? It’s like pulling one thread on a sweater and then watching the sleeve fall off three tiers away.

I asked the league office. The answer was that “this situation is not anticipated.” Every situation is not anticipated, right up until it happens.

I asked my colleague Dave Okonkwo whether New Orleans had any reason to try to finish in a promotion spot it can’t use. “Spite,” he said. “Spite is an incentive.” Then, after a pause: “Also attendance.”

Oakland gets relegated. Oakland plays in the MLB West, and the last-place team in each division goes down. If that happens, the A’s would have two clubs in the ABL and none in MLB, and New Orleans still couldn’t be promoted, because rule No. 2 says it can never be promoted, full stop. The network’s only way back up would run through Oakland winning its way out of the same league as its own sister club. The A’s would have built themselves a cage and then politely locked the door from the outside.

Would Oakland and New Orleans play each other? The rulebook doesn’t say they can’t. I asked one rival executive whether he’d trust the result of Oakland-New Orleans. He laughed for long enough that I’m counting it as an answer.

What is the offseason? This is the one I think we’re going to be arguing about all year.

The rule says players can move freely during the offseason. The rule doesn’t define the offseason. The A’s appear to have concluded that the offseason lasts until the exact moment of the first pitch of the season, and they intend to use every second of it.

Here’s what has happened in the last 36 hours:

  • Mariano Rivera, who spent the winter as Alaska’s closer, is now Oakland’s closer.
  • Brian Giles, who was in New Orleans’ lineup in every projection published this week, is now Oakland’s center fielder.
  • Bernie Williams, Alaska’s leadoff hitter in those same projections, has moved to New Orleans.

Several of this week’s preseason previews went to print with all three in the wrong place. Ours included.

Is the All-Star break an offseason? Is a rainout? Is a rain delay? According to a league source, the A’s asked the league on Thursday whether a trade between two network clubs, as opposed to a transfer, counts as moving a player under rule No. 3. The league said it would get back to them. I’d like to be very clear that I don’t know what the A’s are planning. I’d also like to be very clear that I’m going to be watching the transactions wire every morning until October.

A reader asks

A reader named Kevin, from Fresno, emailed to ask whether one player could appear on all three A’s rosters on the same day.

I’ve thought about this more than is healthy. The answer seems to be yes, as long as it’s the offseason, and as long as the A’s are willing to file the paperwork three times. In the offseason, of course, nobody plays any games. So the player would be on three rosters and play for none of them, which, to be fair, is also a fairly accurate summary of the 2000 season.

We’ve done this before, sort of

The A’s aren’t the first owners to run more than one team at once. They’re the first in a century to do it with permission.

In 1899, the Robison brothers owned both the Cleveland Spiders and the St. Louis Perfectos. They took everyone good from Cleveland, including Cy Young, Jesse Burkett and Bobby Wallace, and moved them to St. Louis. The Spiders went 20-134, which is still the worst record in big-league history and will likely remain so until the end of time.

That same year, the owners of the Baltimore Orioles bought into Brooklyn and moved Willie Keeler, Hughie Jennings and their manager, Ned Hanlon, up the coast. Brooklyn won the pennant in 1899 and again in 1900. Baseball spent the next hundred years treating syndicate ownership as a crime against nature.

There’s a team called the Cleveland Spiders again in this league. It’s in the ABL, the same league as New Orleans. I can’t tell you how badly I want to be in the press box the first time they play.

European soccer has been dealing with a version of this too. In 1998 UEFA decided that two clubs with the same owner can’t play in the same competition, and the Court of Arbitration for Sport backed it up the following year. Our league’s answer was nearly the opposite: the A’s clubs can share an owner and share players, as long as they mostly stay out of each other’s way. I don’t know if that’s wiser. I do know it’s more fun to write about.

Some numbers I can’t stop thinking about

$500,000. That’s what Mariano Rivera will earn this year. John Franco, who will pitch the seventh and eighth innings in front of him, will make $1.2 million. The best reliever alive will make less than half of what his setup man makes because of the specific sequence in which he was drafted, transferred and paid inside a three-team system. I’ve asked four people to explain it to me. I’ve gotten four different explanations, two of which included the word “synergy.”

34. That’s how many saves Rod Beck needs to reach 300. Beck has 266 career saves. Under the new league’s rules, saves in the ABL and FBL count toward career milestones exactly like saves in MLB. Until yesterday, Beck was Alaska’s setup man. Now that Rivera has moved to Oakland, Beck is Alaska’s closer, facing third-tier hitters every night. So the most consequential move this spring for the 300-save club was made in order to change who closes for a different team in a different league.

422. John Franco’s career saves. Lee Smith’s record is 478. I spent an evening I’ll never get back working out the alternate version of this weekend: the A’s send Franco, not Rivera, the other direction. Franco closes in Alaska against FBL lineups for two seasons and finishes his career at 43 as baseball’s all-time saves leader, having never thrown a pitch in the top division again. That was possible as recently as yesterday. As of tomorrow it isn’t, at least until October.

More than 1,000. That’s the number of miles between Alaska’s home park and the nearest FBL opponent, which is in Vancouver. Alaska’s low-minors affiliate is in Edmonton, a different country. So Alaska’s farm system is in Canada, and Alaska is itself, by design, a farm system for Oakland and New Orleans. It’s farms all the way down.

Why do it at all?

The cynical answer is that the A’s wanted to cheat and found a way to make it legal. I don’t think that’s quite right, or at least it isn’t the whole story.

The A’s have always had to find a different way to win, because they’ve never had the money of the teams around them: the Seals, with Silicon Valley behind them, and the Yankees, with everything behind them. Billy Beane’s whole career has been about finding things other teams don’t value. This time the undervalued thing wasn’t on-base percentage or submarine relievers. It was the rulebook.

“Every other team gets one roster to think about,” Beane said this week. “We get to think about three. That’s not cheating. That’s a bigger problem to solve, and we like problems.”

The best argument for the network, though, is a player the A’s didn’t need it to find. St. Louis let Albert Pujols go as an inconsequential minor leaguer before the strike. Oakland took him, and he spent last summer destroying the California league. He’s 22, and he’ll play left field tomorrow for a team that ranks 11th in our preseason projections. That’s lower than I’d put them, mostly because the projections can’t model a front office that treats the calendar as a resource.

Tomorrow

Tomorrow at the first pitch, the cloud condenses. The A’s become three normal baseball teams with three normal rosters, playing three normal schedules in three different leagues, for six months.

I don’t believe that, and I’m not sure they do either.

I’ll leave you with the question I keep coming back to, the one nobody in the league office would answer: if one of the A’s clubs breaks a rule nobody has finished writing, which A’s club gets punished?

The answer, I suspect, is “Alaska.” It’s always Alaska. That’s what Alaska is for.
