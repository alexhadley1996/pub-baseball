FBL · 2002-03-30 · By Ray Dalton, The Houston Gazette

The Killer B's Went South

Craig Biggio and Jeff Bagwell were supposed to finish their careers in Houston. Instead they're in Monterrey, in the third tier, playing for a club nobody back home had heard of two years ago. They say they've never been happier, and it took me a week down here to believe them.

MONTERREY, Mexico — The mountain is the first thing you notice. It’s called the Cerro de la Silla, Saddle Mountain, and it rises over this city like a sleeping animal, gray and jagged and turning orange in the late afternoon light. From center field at the Sultanes’ ballpark you can see it over the left-field wall, and on Thursday evening Craig Biggio stood out there, between fly balls, just looking at it.

“Every day,” he said. “I look at it every day. I’ve lived here a year and a half and I still can’t get over it.”

Then a coach hit him a fly ball, and Biggio, who is 36 years old and has been a catcher, a second baseman and now, apparently, a center fielder, went racing back toward the wall, his helmet so caked with pine tar that it looks like it’s been dipped in molasses. He caught it at a full sprint, slammed into the fence, bounced off and fired the ball back to the infield.

About 200 people had come out just to watch batting practice. They cheered like he’d won a World Series.

I’ve covered Biggio and Jeff Bagwell for 11 years. I’ve watched them carry the Astros through good teams and bad, and through the long, ugly years of the strike. I never imagined I’d need a passport to see them play.

Why

That’s the question everyone in Houston wants answered: why? Why would the two greatest players in franchise history, men who were supposed to retire as Astros and have their numbers hanging over the outfield wall forever, leave for a team in Mexico, in the lowest tier of the new league?

The answer begins in the spring of 2000, and it isn’t a happy one.

When the players walked out that spring, the Astros didn’t wait. Ownership broke ranks almost immediately and started signing players to short, enormous contracts, just as the Yankees were doing in New York. Houston started playing again, first against a handful of new Texas clubs and later in the new Southwest league. A few Astros crossed the line. Tony Clark, who’d come over from Detroit, was one of them.

Biggio and Bagwell didn’t.

“We’re union guys,” Bagwell says, sitting in the dugout with his feet up on the rail. “We were union guys in ‘94 and we were union guys in 2000. I wasn’t going to cross a line and play next to replacement players while the guys I came up with were sitting at home. I couldn’t do it. Neither could Craig.”

Bagwell has a complicated history with strikes. In 1994, he was having one of the best offensive seasons anyone had seen in decades. On August 10, a pitch broke his left hand. Two days later, the players went on strike, and the season never resumed. He won the MVP anyway, unanimously, for a season that ended twice in the same week.

“So I’ve had my season taken away before,” he says, and shrugs. “I wasn’t going to help them take it away from somebody else.”

The winter that didn’t end

In the late fall of 2000, a few weeks after the Supreme Court ruling, the phone rang in Biggio’s house in Houston. It was a man from Monterrey, representing the Sultanes, one of the oldest and proudest clubs in Mexican baseball. Mexican winter ball had never been part of the dispute in the States, and the man had a proposal. Come down for the winter, play six weeks, make some money and stay in shape. Bring your friend.

“I called Jeff,” Biggio says. “I said, ‘You want to go to Mexico for a month?’ He said, ‘Sure.’ That was the whole conversation.”

They came for six weeks. They never left.

It’s not hard to understand why, once you’ve been here. Monterrey is an industrial city of about 3 million people, tucked against the Sierra Madre, and it is completely, unapologetically crazy about baseball. The Sultanes have played here since the 1930s. Their fans call them Los Fantasmas Grises, the Gray Ghosts. Every home game feels like a party: a brass band in the upper deck, families sharing roast goat in the concourse and children wearing Biggio jerseys that, as far as I can tell, were made locally and never licensed by anyone.

“In Houston, we were good players on a good team,” Bagwell says. “Here, I go to the grocery store and people bring their kids over to shake my hand. I don’t mean that in a bragging way. I mean I’ve never felt that before in my life.”

The locals call them “los muchachos de Nueva Inglaterra,” the New England boys. That’s not quite accurate, since Bagwell grew up in Connecticut and Biggio on Long Island, but nobody here is interested in the correction, and neither are they.

Last summer, the Sultanes played Mexico City, with Jim Thome and Adam Dunn hitting home runs into the thin air of the capital, in what became the most-watched game in the history of Mexican television. More than 26,000 people crammed into the ballpark here. Millions more watched across the country. Bagwell hit two home runs. Biggio was hit by two pitches, which, if you’ve watched his career, is the most Biggio stat imaginable.

“That night,” Biggio says, “I knew we weren’t going back.”

The team they left behind

Meanwhile, back in Houston, the Astros were doing fine without them, which is the part that still stings.

Houston signed Sammy Sosa and Alex Rodriguez to short, massive contracts. The Astros won the Southwest league, then played the San Francisco Seals in January in a nine-game “West Coast World Series” that drew more than half a million fans. The ballpark downtown, which until last month was named after a company that no longer exists, was full nearly every night.

Did it hurt to watch?

Biggio doesn’t answer right away.

“I’m a Houston guy,” he says. “My kids were born there. I still have a house there. I’ll always be a Houston guy. So yeah, sure, it hurt. It hurt to see them win without us. But it would’ve hurt more to be part of the way they did it.”

The Astros, to their credit, tried to bring them back. When the new league formed in January, Drayton McLane offered each of them a one-year deal to come home. People familiar with the offers say they were generous. McLane, reached this week, was gracious about all of it.

“I love those two,” he says. “I always will. The door in Houston is always open, and they know that.”

Here is the twist, though. Bagwell said no and signed a six-year contract with Monterrey, which takes him into his late 30s. Biggio said no too, but he took the league’s one-year arbitrated deal. He’ll be a free agent again next winter.

I asked them about the difference. Bagwell answered first.

“I’m done moving,” he said. “I’ve got a house here now. My wife likes it here. I like it here. This is where I’m going to finish.”

Biggio was quiet for a while.

“I’m 36,” he finally said. “At 36, everybody goes year to year. I don’t know where I’ll be next year. I know where I’ll be this year.”

Is there any chance he goes home?

“There’s always a chance,” he said. “But I’ve learned something about baseball in the last two years. You don’t know anything. Two years ago I didn’t know where Monterrey was.”

The third tier

There’s one more thing that bothers people here, and it bothers them a lot.

When the new league sorted its 60 clubs into three tiers, Monterrey ended up at the bottom, in the FBL, despite the TV ratings, despite the crowds, and despite having two future Hall of Famers on its roster. Talk to anyone connected to the Sultanes and they’ll tell you the same thing: the old American owners never took Mexican baseball seriously, and the sorting proved it.

“The most-watched game in Mexican history,” says Vinny Castilla, the Sultanes’ third baseman and, as a native of Oaxaca, probably the most beloved baseball player in this country. “And they put us at the bottom. That’s fine. We’ll go up. Every year we go up, and we’ll see who laughs.”

Biggio and Bagwell have a simpler way of looking at it.

“Two promotions,” Bagwell says. “That’s all. Two good seasons and we’re in MLB. And then guess who comes to Houston as the visiting team?”

He doesn’t say it with any bitterness. If anything, he seems delighted by the idea.

The roster

Let’s be honest about the Sultanes, though, because the experts certainly have been. One preseason ranking put Monterrey 13th of the 20 FBL clubs and noted, fairly, that Bagwell and Biggio “are still studs” but that the lineup is “aging out fast.” The projections have the Sultanes ninth of 10 in the FBL’s West Group, at 70-81.

The rotation is young and inexperienced, built around 25-year-old Bronson Arroyo, 22-year-old Jon Garland and 23-year-old Chris Capuano, and it’s going to give up runs. The bullpen is another story. Mike Remlinger, a veteran left-hander, is the setup man, and the closer is a 20-year-old Venezuelan named Francisco Rodriguez, who throws harder than anyone I’ve seen this spring. One scout told me he has “the best arm I’ve seen in Mexico, and maybe anywhere.”

“If we can get leads to the kid,” Biggio says, “we’re going to win a lot of games.”

And then there’s Biggio himself, playing center field. He’s a catcher by trade who became an All-Star second baseman, and now he’s chasing fly balls in Mexico at 36, because the Sultanes needed someone to do it.

“I’ve played three positions in the big leagues,” he says. “What’s one more? You put me where you need me. That’s the job.”

Monday

The Sultanes open the FBL season Monday night at home against the San Antonio Rebeldes, a club built around Mexican-American culture on the other side of the border. People here are calling it a derby, even though the two cities are 300 miles apart and play in different countries. It’s sold out.

On Thursday night, after batting practice, I walked out of the ballpark with Bagwell. A crowd was waiting at the gate, mostly kids, and he stopped and signed every single thing they held up: balls, programs, scraps of paper, a little girl’s arm. A man in the crowd yelled something in Spanish that made everybody laugh.

I asked Bagwell what he’d said.

“He said, ‘Don’t ever go back to Texas,’” Bagwell said, grinning. “I hear that a lot.”

What does he tell them?

“I tell them I’m not going anywhere.”

Then he got in his car and drove home, through a city that two years ago he couldn’t have found on a map, toward the mountain that looks like a saddle in the evening light.
