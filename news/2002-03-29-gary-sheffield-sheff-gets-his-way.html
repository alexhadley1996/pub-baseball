FBL · 2002-03-29 · By Frank DeLuca, New York Evening Star

Sheff Gets His Way

Gary Sheffield wanted two things: respect and shortstop. Hartford gave him both, plus a contract nobody else in baseball would have written.

HARTFORD, Conn. — Here is Gary Sheffield, 33 years old, taking ground balls at shortstop on a cold March morning in Connecticut, which is something nobody in baseball thought they’d see again.

He backhands one in the hole and throws a strike to first. He charges a slow roller, barehands it and throws on the run. He boots the next one, says something to himself that I can’t print in a family newspaper, and waves for another.

“Again,” he says.

The coach hits it again. Sheffield fields it clean.

“That’s the one they’ll remember,” he says, walking off the field. “Not the other one. The other one doesn’t count.”

The other one always counts. That’s the thing about shortstop. And it’s the thing about Gary Sheffield, who has spent his whole career being told what counts and what doesn’t by people he didn’t think had earned the right.

Where he came from

You know the résumé.

He came out of Tampa, the nephew of Doc Gooden, a kid with fast hands and a temper. The Brewers made him the sixth pick in the 1986 draft as a shortstop. He made the big leagues at 19, as a shortstop. Then Milwaukee moved him to third base, and later everybody moved him to the outfield, and nobody ever let him go back.

He won a batting title in San Diego. He won a World Series in Florida in 1997. He was traded to Los Angeles in the Mike Piazza deal and hit there the way he’s always hit: the bat waving back and forth before every pitch like a man trying to scare off a wasp, and then the ball leaving the yard.

Over the last two years he’s hit 79 home runs. That’s how many Barry Bonds hit last year by himself, which is the kind of thing Sheffield does not want to talk about and which I’m bringing up anyway.

The war

When the Dodgers broke the line during the strike and started playing in the California league, Sheffield went with them. He hit. They won. Everybody made money.

And then, in the spring of 2001, it all came apart.

The details depend on who you ask. Sheffield says the Dodgers promised him a new contract and didn’t deliver. The Dodgers say Sheffield wanted too much and said too much, in public, about the people who signed his checks. Both sides are telling some version of the truth, which is how these things usually go.

“I’m not going to stay where I’m not respected,” Sheffield says. “I’ve done that before. I’m not doing it again.”

The call

Hartford is a long way from Los Angeles. Hartford is a long way from everywhere, if we’re being honest, including New York, which is about two hours down I-91 and has been looking down on Hartford for as long as anyone can remember.

In the summer of 2000, the Yard Goats were a breakaway minor league club, one of the four teams in the Yankees’ little mini-league. The Yankees beat them like a drum. They beat everybody like a drum. Hartford didn’t have stars. Hartford had a downtown ballpark, a funny name and an owner named Jack Bruner who wanted to do things nobody else in baseball was doing.

A yard goat, if you were wondering, is the little railroad engine that pushes cars around the train yard. It doesn’t go anywhere fancy. It just works.

When Bruner heard Sheffield and the Dodgers were finished, he made one phone call.

“I asked him what he wanted,” Bruner says. “He said, ‘Respect.’ I said, ‘What else?’ He said, ‘Shortstop.’ I said, ‘OK.’”

That was the negotiation.

The contract

Here’s where it gets interesting.

Sheffield’s deal with Hartford pays him $6.7 million this year. That’s a lot of money for a club in the third tier, but it isn’t the money that has people around baseball talking. It’s the clauses.

The first one, according to people who have seen the contract, says that if Hartford ever moves Sheffield off shortstop, for any reason other than injury, he becomes a free agent that day. Not at the end of the season. That day.

“Experimental,” Bruner calls it. Bruner has said from the start that he wants to try contracts nobody else will try.

“Every other team writes contracts that protect the team,” he says. “I wanted to write one that protected the player’s dignity. If I’m wrong about him at shortstop, I deserve to lose him.”

What does Sheffield think?

“I think it’s the smartest contract in baseball,” he says. “Because I’m not coming off shortstop.”

The second consequence of the clause is the one rival executives have noticed. The Teams to Watch preview this spring suggested that if Hartford falls out of the race, Sheffield and Ellis Burks “may become midseason trade dominoes.” Fine. Any team that trades for Sheffield gets the clause too. You want Gary Sheffield’s bat for your stretch run? Congratulations. He’s your shortstop.

“That’s a poison pill,” one AL executive told me. “I’ve never seen anything like it. You’d have to really, really want him.”

Somewhere in the Bronx, I suspect, George Steinbrenner read that sentence and said, “We really, really want him.”

The team

Here’s the thing about the Yard Goats. They can hit.

One preseason ranking put Hartford fourth in the FBL and gave the offense a B-plus: “Veteran thump (Sheffield/Burks) plus legitimate prospect intrigue.” Burks is 37 and still has a swing that ought to come with a warning label. Ray Durham and Randy Winn get on base. Jose Guillen is 25 and has the kind of raw power that makes scouts write “if” in big letters, and Adam LaRoche is 22 and looks like a hitter.

The pitching is another story. The same ranking gave Hartford a C-minus for its staff, calling it “experienced but flawed,” with too many walks and not enough strikeouts. Joe Mays doesn’t miss bats. Chuck Finley is 39. The preview noted, politely, that Hartford’s defense “projects older and slower,” which is a nice way of saying that a 33-year-old who hasn’t played shortstop regularly in more than a decade will be playing shortstop every day.

“I’ve heard it,” Sheffield says. “Older and slower. I heard it when I was 25, too. People’ve been telling me what I can’t do my whole life.”

Monday

The Yard Goats open the 2002 season Monday in Honolulu.

Read that again. The Hartford Yard Goats, from the capital of Connecticut, open the season in Hawaii, against a provisional expansion club run by a man who never played baseball. Sheffield is very much looking forward to it.

“I’ve never been to Hawaii,” he says. “I’m going to hit one into the ocean.”

The ballpark in Honolulu isn’t near the ocean.

“Then I’ll hit it farther.”

He’ll make errors at shortstop this year. That’s guaranteed. He’ll make a lot of them, and every one will get written up as evidence that Hartford made a mistake. And he’ll hit 35 or 40 home runs, because he always does, and every one of those will be evidence of something too.

Here’s what I think. I think Gary Sheffield found the one owner in baseball willing to give him what he actually wanted, which was never just money. It was to be taken at his word.

That’s rarer than a shortstop with 40-homer power.
