MLB · 2002-03-30 · By Thomas Whitcomb, The Washington Ledger

The Son Who Runs

Washington has waited 30 years for a team in baseball's top tier. Tim Raines Jr. waited 22 years to get out from behind his father's shadow. Starting Sunday, neither of them has to wait anymore.

MIAMI — Speed is the only thing in baseball that doesn’t slump. Hitters slump, pitchers lose their release point, and even great glove men go through weeks when the ball seems to find their shins. Legs don’t do that. A fast man is fast on Opening Day and fast in the dog days of August, until one spring, all at once, he isn’t fast anymore, and he never will be again.

Tim Raines Jr. is 22 years old, and he’s fast. His father, Tim Raines, is 42 years old, and he isn’t.

That, in a sentence, is the story of how the son ended up a Washington Cherry Blossom and the father ended up somewhere else. It’s also, if you squint, the story of baseball itself, which is always asking the young to run for the old and is never quite sure what to do with the old once they’ve stopped running.

The family business

For most of the 1980s, nobody in the National League ran like Tim Raines. With the Montreal Expos he stole more than 70 bases for six straight seasons, and he finished his career with 808, at the time behind only Rickey Henderson, Lou Brock, Billy Hamilton and Ty Cobb. In the 1987 All-Star Game he won it with a triple in the 13th inning, and everyone in the ballpark understood that was the only way a man like Raines should ever win anything: running, as fast as the rules allowed.

His son was born in Memphis in 1979, when the father was still a minor leaguer with a duffel bag and a dream. Little Tim grew up in clubhouses and spring training complexes, playing catch with grown men whose names were on the backs of baseball cards. The Orioles drafted him in 1998. Everybody who saw him play said the same thing: the kid could fly, just like his dad.

Last winter, when the old leagues collapsed and the new one rose out of the rubble, the Raineses had a plan. They wanted to sign together. Father and son, same club, same dugout, maybe even the same outfield on a summer night when the manager was feeling sentimental. Ken Griffey and Ken Griffey Jr. had done it in Seattle, and baseball had cried a little.

Nobody would take both.

The Cherry Blossoms, who’d spent all of 2001 building themselves in the image of the ’80s Cardinals (defense, speed, small ball and the nerve to make the other team field it), wanted the son. They didn’t want a 42-year-old father, even one who’d spent a decade chasing those same Cardinals for the Expos. Detroit, rebuilt from almost nothing and eager to take anybody who could still hit, wanted the father. It didn’t need the son.

So they split up, and the father told the son to take the deal.

“He said, ‘They picked you for your legs,’” Raines Jr. recalled this week, sitting in the lobby of the team hotel here the night before Opening Day. “‘Don’t you ever let them regret it.’”

A town without a team

To understand why one 22-year-old pinch runner matters to this city, you have to understand what Washington has been missing.

The first Senators left for Minnesota after 1960. The second Senators left for Texas after 1971. That last night at RFK, Sept. 30, 1971, Washington led the Yankees 7-5 with two outs in the ninth when the fans, unable to stand it any longer, poured out of the stands and onto the field. The game was forfeited to New York. Washington’s last act as a big-league city was to lose a game it was winning because it loved the team too much to let it go.

For 30 years after that, the city’s baseball was something you drove to, 40 miles up the parkway to Baltimore, or something you remembered. Our grandfathers told us about Walter Johnson. Our fathers told us about Frank Howard. We told our children, “First in war, first in peace, and last in the American League,” and then we explained that it used to be a joke about a team that existed.

Then came the strike, and the collapse, and last spring a brand-new team with a ridiculous, wonderful name, playing in Atlanta’s new Southern League. It was baseball, and the city took it gratefully. But it wasn’t the big leagues, and everyone knew it.

On Sunday afternoon in Miami, it is. For the first time since that last night at RFK, Washington has a team in the top division of the sport. If you’re scoring at home, the Orioles are in the second tier this year. For the first time in history, Washington outranks Baltimore. Don’t tell anyone in Baltimore I said so.

The 25th man

Raines Jr. won’t start Sunday, and he knows it. On this roster, he’s an extra outfielder, a defensive replacement and, most of all, a pinch runner. He’s the guy manager Travis Sherwood will point to in the eighth inning of a one-run game with a slow man on first.

Some players would sulk about that. Raines Jr. talks about it as though he’s been handed the keys to the city.

“Think about it,” he says. “There’s one guy on the bench whose whole job is to change the game in 90 feet. That’s me. I don’t have to get a hit. I don’t have to catch anything. I just have to be faster than the catcher’s arm. And I’ve been faster than catchers’ arms since I was 6.”

There’s an old idea in baseball that the pinch runner is the purest specialist in the sport. He enters the game without a bat or a glove, with nothing but his legs and his nerve, and he has one job, which everyone in the ballpark knows he’s there to do. The pitcher knows it. The catcher knows it. Thirty thousand people know it. And still he has to go.

“The hard part’s not the running,” Raines Jr. says. “The hard part’s that everybody knows. My dad says the best steals are the ones where everybody knows and you go anyway.”

That’s what the Cherry Blossoms are built for. The scouts are skeptical; one preseason ranking puts Washington 16th of the 20 MLB clubs and grades its pitching staff a D. The computer projections, on the other hand, love the strikeouts from young Johan Santana, the defense, and the general idea of a team that makes the other side work for every out. They have Washington winning 86 games and tied for the best record in the East.

Somewhere between the scouts and the computers is the truth, and somewhere in the middle of that truth is a kid who will spend his summer sitting on the end of the bench, waiting for the eighth inning.

Memorial Day

The father, reached by phone this week, laughed the big, rolling laugh that’s been echoing through clubhouses for a quarter of a century.

“Do I wish we were together? Sure,” Tim Raines said. “But I’m 42, man. I’m not stealing 70 bases. Washington needed somebody who could. They got the right Raines.”

He’ll play left field for Detroit, maybe a little first base, and hit somewhere in the middle of the order, which would’ve sounded absurd for most of his career and sounds perfectly sensible now. The legs are the first thing to go. The eye is the last. Raines still has the eye.

The schedule has given the family a date. Washington goes to Detroit for three games on Memorial Day weekend, May 26 through 28. It will be the first time in professional baseball that Tim Raines has ever stood on the same field as his son and wanted him to lose.

“Oh, I want him to lose,” the father says, laughing again. “I want him to lose every game but one. And in that one, I want him to steal second on our catcher and have the whole stadium boo him.”

What will he do if his son steals on him?

“I’ll be in left field,” Raines says. “I’ll clap. Nobody’ll see me. That’s fine.”

The blossoms

A thousand miles north of here, along the Tidal Basin, the cherry trees are starting to open. If the Park Service is right, they’ll be close to full bloom when the Cherry Blossoms play their first game in Washington on Tuesday night against Seattle, at the new ballpark by the Anacostia.

There’s something right about that, and something a little sad. The blossoms last about two weeks. Then the petals blow across the water, the tourists go home, and it’s summer, long and hot and full of ordinary days.

Speed is like that too. It arrives all at once, in a young man’s legs, and it is glorious, and everyone stops to look. Then one spring it’s gone, and the young man is 42 and playing left field in Detroit, laughing on the phone about the son who has it now.

Washington waited 30 years for this. Tim Raines Jr. waited 22.

On Sunday, somewhere around the eighth inning, with a slow man on first and the game on the line, a manager in a brand-new uniform will look down the bench and point. A 22-year-old will stand up and jog out to first base. Everybody in the ballpark will know what he’s there to do.

He’ll go anyway. That’s the whole point.
