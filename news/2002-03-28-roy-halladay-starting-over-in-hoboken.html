FBL · 2002-03-28 · By Paul Ricci, Sporting Weekly

Starting Over in Hoboken

Two years ago, Roy Halladay had the worst season any pitcher had ever had. Hoboken signed him anyway. Now some people think he might be the best pitcher in the FBL. He'd like everyone to slow down.

HOBOKEN, N.J. — The number is 10.64.

Roy Halladay doesn’t say it out loud. Nobody around him says it either. But it’s there, the way a scar is there, and if you spend enough time with him you start to understand that everything he does now — the 5 a.m. workouts, the notebooks, the silence on the days he pitches — is in some way an answer to it.

In the summer of 2000, pitching for a brand-new team in a brand-new league, Halladay allowed 107 hits and 80 earned runs in 67⅔ innings. That’s an ERA of 10.64, the worst anyone had ever posted over that many innings. He was 23, a former first-round pick, and two years removed from coming within one out of a no-hitter in only his second big-league start.

Everybody who saw him that summer had the same thought. He was finished.

Almost everybody.

The believer

Stacy Anastasia is 61, grew up a few blocks from here, and runs the baseball side of the Hoboken Zephyrs with the same fierce, slightly suspicious affection she brings to everything in her hometown. She’ll remind you, if you give her the opening, that the first recorded game of organized baseball was played here, at the Elysian Fields, in 1846. She’ll also remind you that nobody in New York has ever taken Hoboken seriously, and that she has spent her whole life not caring.

In July 2000, when the Zephyrs were thrown together as one of the four clubs in the Yankees’ mini-league, she needed arms. Toronto, waiting out the strike with no games to play, was willing to part with a tall, struggling right-hander for almost nothing.

“I remembered him,” Anastasia says. “September 1998. Second start of his career, against Detroit. He had a no-hitter with two outs in the ninth, and Bobby Higginson hit a pinch-hit home run. I was watching on TV. I never forgot it. You don’t almost throw a no-hitter by accident.”

(Higginson, for what it’s worth, now plays for Steve Cohen’s Brooklyn Gothams, just across the river. Halladay says he hasn’t thought about that. Nobody believes him.)

So Hoboken signed Halladay, and then he spent that summer getting destroyed.

The fastball was flat. The curveball hung. He walked people and then gave up hits to the people he didn’t walk. Lineups that had been stocked overnight with big leaguers who’d crossed the line treated him like batting practice. By the end of August the Zephyrs’ own coaches were privately telling Anastasia to cut him.

“Everybody told me,” she says. “My manager, my coaches, my accountant. My accountant had opinions about pitching. And I said no. I said, ‘This kid is lost. He’s not bad. There’s a difference.’”

The rebuild

What happened next is where this story becomes something more than a story about a good pitcher.

Instead of releasing Halladay, Hoboken sent him away. In the spring of 2001, the club hired Mel Queen, a longtime pitching instructor with a reputation for taking pitchers apart and rebuilding them, as a consultant. They sent Halladay to a back field in Florida, far from the Zephyrs and far from New York, with instructions to start over.

“Mel said, ‘Forget everything you know,’” Halladay remembers. “Everything. Delivery, arm angle, the way I thought about pitching. Start from zero.”

Queen dropped Halladay’s arm angle from straight over the top to a lower three-quarters slot. The fastball, which had been straight and hittable, started to sink and run. Halladay developed a cutter that broke late, in on lefties’ hands. He started over in the bullpen, against low-level hitters, just trying to throw the ball where he meant to.

The other half of the rebuild came from his wife, Brandy. She found a book by the sports psychologist Harvey Dorfman, “The Mental ABC’s of Pitching,” and handed it to him one night.

“He read it like four times,” Brandy says. “He’d underline stuff. He started keeping notebooks. He’s always been intense, but he became a different kind of intense. It was like before, he was fighting himself. After, he was just fighting the hitter.”

When Halladay came back to Hoboken in the middle of the 2001 season, he was unrecognizable.

In the second half of the tri-state league, against the 100-18 Yankees, the Knights, Brooklyn and Hartford, he threw 105⅓ innings with a 3.16 ERA. He struck out 96, walked 25 and allowed three home runs. Three, in more than 100 innings, in a league where Barry Bonds hit 79.

Compare that with 1999, before everything went wrong, when he walked 79 batters in 149 innings. His walk rate fell by more than half. The pitcher who couldn’t find the plate now seemed to live on the corners.

“The first time I caught him after he came back,” says Mike Kinkade, one of the Zephyrs’ catchers, “I set up away, and the ball just went there. And then I set up in, and it went there. I remember thinking, who is this guy?”

The skeptics

Here’s where I’m obligated to tell you to slow down. Halladay would want me to.

The 2001 numbers are a half-season, 105 innings, in a regional league of uncertain quality. The tri-state league had the Yankees, who were very, very good, and three other clubs that ranged from respectable to disastrous. Halladay pitched well against all of them. That still isn’t the same as a full season, and it certainly isn’t the same as a career.

“I like him a lot,” one AL scout who has seen Halladay this spring said. “The sinker is legit. The cutter is legit. The command is a hundred times better. But I’ve seen a lot of guys have a great half-season and then go right back to being who they were. Ask me in September.”

A second scout was more optimistic. “That’s a No. 1 starter,” he said flatly. “In this league or any league. He’s got three plus pitches and he throws strikes. The only question is whether his head stays where it is right now.”

That’s the real question, and everyone in Hoboken knows it. Halladay’s 2000 collapse wasn’t just mechanical. He lost confidence, then he lost the strike zone, and the two fed each other until there was nothing left. The rebuild fixed the mechanics. Whether it permanently fixed the rest is something nobody can know yet, including Halladay.

“I’m not anything yet,” he says, when I ask whether he’s an ace. “I had a good half. That’s it. I’ve had bad halves too. I’d like to string a few good ones together before anybody calls me anything.”

The bottom of the totem pole

The Zephyrs open the season in the FBL, the third tier. That’s partly a reflection of where the new league’s founders thought Hoboken belonged and partly a reflection of the roster, which is thin almost everywhere except the rotation.

One preseason ranking put Hoboken 15th in the FBL, with one of the weaker lineups in the league. Michael Young, a 25-year-old shortstop, and Vernon Wells, a 23-year-old center fielder, are interesting, and that’s about it. But the same ranking called Halladay, Livan Hernandez and Carlos Silva “a strong young trio.” One preseason awards preview listed Halladay among three FBL Cy Young contenders, alongside Curt Schilling and Roger Clemens.

That’s company Halladay doesn’t want to talk about.

“Those guys have won a lot of games,” he says. “I haven’t.”

There is also, inevitably, Brooklyn. Steve Cohen’s Gothams play in MLB and spent the winter trying to buy talent from anyone who’d sell. According to people familiar with the conversations, Cohen made at least two offers for Halladay. Anastasia turned down both.

“He’s ours,” she says. “We believed in him when nobody else did. I’m not selling that to a hedge fund manager across the river who wants a replica of a replica.”

(The Zephyrs, it should be noted, play in a replica of Ebbets Field, which irritates the Gothams to no end.)

Opening Day

Manager Evan Griffith has named Halladay the Opening Day starter on Monday, at home, against Memphis.

The forecast calls for cold. Halladay doesn’t care about the cold. He cares about the routine: the long toss at a certain time, the bullpen at a certain time, not talking to anybody, including Brandy, for the last few hours before he pitches.

“He doesn’t talk to me on game days,” Brandy says, laughing. “It used to hurt my feelings. Now I think it’s sweet. It means he’s focused. It means he’s back.”

Back in 1998, a Toronto broadcaster gave Halladay a nickname, “Doc,” after the Old West gunslinger. It never really caught on in Hoboken. Halladay seems to prefer it that way.

“I’ll take a nickname when I earn one,” he says.

On Thursday afternoon, after his last bullpen session of the spring, Halladay walked past a small plaque near the ballpark entrance, the one that commemorates that first game at the Elysian Fields in 1846. He stopped and read it, the way people do when they’ve walked past something a hundred times and never really looked.

“Baseball started here,” he said. “That’s pretty cool.”

Then he walked on.

I thought about saying something about second beginnings, about baseball starting here twice. I didn’t. Halladay would have rolled his eyes.

Monday, against Memphis, he’ll find out what he is. So will the rest of us.
