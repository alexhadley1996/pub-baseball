ABL · 2002-03-31 · By Daniel Hale, The Sunday Magazine

The New New Team

Everyone agrees the Portland Beavers will finish last. Jake Kirk, the Microsoft executive who owns them, thinks everyone is making the same mistake, and he has a spreadsheet that says so.

I’d planned to spend this baseball season with the Oakland A’s. Billy Beane, it turned out, now had three teams to run and no time for a writer following him around. So in February, on the advice of a friend in Seattle, I flew north to spend a few weeks with the team that every expert in baseball agrees is the worst in its league. My friend’s only explanation was that the owner was “the most interesting man in baseball who isn’t in baseball.”

Jake Kirk is 44, thin, and wears the same gray fleece vest every day. He is an Oregon native who spent most of the 1990s at Microsoft, on the team that built Excel, which may explain why he thinks about baseball the way other people think about a spreadsheet that has a bug in it somewhere. When the players walked out before the 2000 season, Kirk didn’t wait to see how it ended. At the first sniff of the strike he founded a professional team in his home state, named it after Portland’s old minor league club, and has been, in his words, “running a very expensive experiment ever since.”

The experiment has a hypothesis. Everyone else in baseball looks at his roster and sees the worst team in the American Baseball League. Kirk looks at it and sees a pricing error.

The building nobody wanted

Before we get to the baseball, we have to talk about the building, because it tells you most of what you need to know about Jake Kirk.

In the late 1990s, a group of investors started building a domed stadium on Portland’s east side, betting the city would land an NFL team. The team never came. The money ran out, the concrete shell sat half-finished, and by early 2000 the city was debating whether to tear it down. Then the strike hit, and a holding company controlled by Kirk bought the whole thing for a figure that people close to the deal describe as “embarrassingly close to a dollar.”

“Everyone saw a building that was worth less than nothing,” Kirk says. “I saw 59,000 seats with a roof, in a city where it rains 150 days a year, that someone was about to pay to destroy. That’s not a bad building. That’s a mispriced building.”

He finished it cheaply, and it looks like it. It’s gray, cavernous and about as charming as a parking garage, and Portlanders have spent two years complaining about it. They’ve also filled it.

“I know how it looks,” Kirk says. “But the alternative was spending $300 million on a beautiful ballpark before I knew whether the team was any good. This way I find out first.”

The projection problem

Every preseason forecast has the Beavers finishing last. The most widely circulated projection has them at 64-87, last in the ABL West. One preseason ranking put them 19th out of 20, with a D for offense and a D for pitching. A popular preview called them “the odds-on relegation pick.” Kirk has read every one, and he has a theory about why they’re all wrong. It’s the theory I came to Portland to hear.

The theory goes like this. Every projection this spring has to translate statistics from 2001, when baseball was played in half a dozen regional leagues of wildly varying quality. Veterans who spent 2001 in the strongest regional leagues have big, recent numbers that the projection systems take mostly at face value. Young players who spent 2001 in the minor leagues have numbers that get discounted, heavily, to account for the weaker competition.

“So you’ve got two kinds of uncertainty,” Kirk says, pulling up a chart on his laptop, a habit I came to recognize. “One kind gets punished, and the other kind gets rewarded. A 34-year-old who hit .300 in the Mountain league gets projected like a .300 hitter. A 24-year-old who hit .300 in Double-A gets projected like a .250 hitter. Both of those are guesses. But the system trusts the first guess and doubts the second.”

He scrolls down.

“Now look at my roster.”

His roster is mostly young players other teams had stopped valuing. Marlon Byrd and Nick Punto came out of a Phillies organization that lost everyone during the strike. Morgan Ensberg was a Houston prospect the Astros forgot about while they were busy buying Sammy Sosa and Alex Rodriguez. Chin-Feng Chen, a 24-year-old outfielder from Taiwan, came from a Dodgers system that was too busy playing winter ball in the California league to notice him. In every case, their 2001 numbers came from the minors, and in every case the projections took them apart.

“Everybody else is buying the safe-looking veterans,” Kirk says. “Those are expensive. Nobody’s buying the scary-looking kids. Those are cheap. If the projections are wrong in the direction I think they are, I’ve bought a lot of underpriced assets.”

And if he’s wrong?

“Then I’ll be relegated, and I’ll have learned something,” he says. “That’s the experiment.”

The innings that matter

The second part of Kirk’s theory concerns the end of the game.

The Beavers’ starting rotation is, by any honest measure, terrible. Its ace, Dave Williams, is 23 and bounced around the minors last season. Steve Parris is 34 and coming off a season of alarming walk rates. Salvador Arellano is 19, from Mexico, with a big fastball and almost no idea where it’s going. Nobody in Portland disputes any of this.

What Kirk did was take the money he might have spent on starting pitching and spend it on the bullpen instead. He has two closers. Troy Percival spent most of the last decade closing games for the Angels. Eric Gagne spent most of it failing as a starter for the Dodgers.

The Gagne story is the one Kirk likes to tell. Last winter, when Gagne was looking for a new team, Montreal offered him a rotation spot and a homecoming. Kirk had his analysts, a group of former Microsoft data people who sit in a windowless room under the dome’s upper deck, pull every pitch Gagne had thrown in the previous three seasons.

“His first time through a lineup, he was one of the best pitchers in baseball,” Kirk says. “His second time through, he was ordinary. His third time through, he was a disaster. Everyone looked at that and said, ‘He’s a failed starter.’ We looked at it and said, ‘He’s a great one-inning pitcher who’s been miscast for four years.’”

So Kirk offered him something Montreal didn’t: the ninth inning. Gagne took it.

The logic underneath is simple enough that it surprised me no one else seemed to be following it. Not all innings matter equally. A run allowed in the first inning of a game you’re losing 6-0 is almost worthless. A run allowed in the ninth inning of a one-run game might decide it. If you can’t afford good pitchers for all nine innings, Kirk reasons, you should buy the ones who pitch the innings that matter most.

“The whole sport is organized around the first inning,” he says. “The best pitcher starts the game. That’s tradition, and it’s backwards. You put your best pitcher where the leverage is.”

Adrian Crawley, the Beavers’ manager, hears this kind of thing every day. He’s 45, a former minor league infielder who looks a little like the father of somebody on the team. He seems to have made his peace with it.

“Jake gives me a lot of numbers,” he says. “Most of them tell me things not to do. Don’t bunt. Don’t hit-and-run. Don’t walk anybody on purpose. Don’t let the third-best pitcher pitch the ninth. I don’t love all of them. But I’ll tell you something. I’ve managed a lot of teams that did all those things, and they weren’t any good either.”

The skeptics

Not everyone is impressed, and in fairness to the skeptics, the record of rich outsiders who think they’ve figured out baseball isn’t an encouraging one.

“I like Jake,” one ABL general manager told me. “He’s smart. But I’ve been doing this for 25 years, and I’ve watched a lot of smart people learn the same lesson. You can’t spreadsheet your way out of a bad starting rotation. The bullpen’s great if you’re ever ahead in the seventh. They won’t be.”

That’s the question at the center of this experiment, and Kirk knows it. Even if he’s right that his young position players are better than their projections and that his relievers are deployed correctly, none of it matters if his starters can’t keep the game close long enough for Gagne and Percival to pitch.

“We’ll be behind a lot,” Kirk admits. “The question is by how much.”

The skeptics also expect the Beavers to sell. If Portland is out of it by July, the conventional thinking goes, the smart move is to trade Gagne and Percival to contenders at peak value and rebuild. One preview this spring raised it as the central strategic question of Portland’s season.

Kirk laughs when I bring it up.

“We’re not going to sell the thing that’s working,” he says. “The whole premise of the experiment is that the market’s wrong about what’s valuable. Selling into the market’s opinion would sort of undermine the premise.”

Opening Day

The Beavers open the season Monday at home, against Nashville.

That’s a fitting opponent. The Nashville Stars are the only other club in the ABL whose manager seems to be running an experiment of his own. Kirk has been looking forward to it for weeks.

“Two teams that think differently,” he says. “Everyone else is going to be watching the Yankees or Bonds or whoever. I’ll be watching the one game in the league where both teams are trying to figure out what the other one knows.”

On my last night in Portland, I sat with Kirk in the empty dome, high in the upper deck, looking down at a field that, by any sensible accounting, shouldn’t exist. The grounds crew was painting the foul lines. The roof, which everyone in Portland loves to complain about, kept the rain off. Kirk had his laptop open, as he always does, and was running one more simulation.

I asked him what it said.

“It says we finish fourth,” he said. “Plus or minus a lot.”

The projections say last. Kirk’s spreadsheet says fourth. One of them has a bug in it somewhere.

“That’s the fun part,” Kirk says, closing the laptop. “In about six months, we find out which one.”
