MLB · 2002-05-14 · By Hal Brennan, Diamond Daily

Ninety Miles, and Then Some

Jose Contreras was the best pitcher in Cuba. He left everything to pitch in America. Now he's 1-6 with a 7.08 ERA for the worst team in MLB, and the people around him are trying to figure out whether the problem is his arm, his head or his heart.

ATLANTA — Taped to the inside of Jose Contreras’s locker, above the hook where he hangs his jacket, is a photograph of two little girls in matching dresses, squinting into the sun on a beach. He doesn’t point it out. You notice it because, after a bad start, he sits facing it for a long time.

Saturday at Wrigley Field was a bad start. Contreras lasted 3⅓ innings against the Cubs, walked five, allowed seven runs and left with the Braves trailing. Atlanta came back to make it close and lost 6-7. Afterward, Contreras sat at his locker with a towel around his neck and stared at the photograph. Nobody bothered him.

“Those are his daughters,” Geronimo Gil, his catcher, said quietly. “They’re in Cuba. He hasn’t seen them since he left.”

The Titan of Bronze

In Cuba, they called him El Titán de Bronce, the Titan of Bronze. For most of the last decade, he was the ace of the Cuban national team, a 6-foot-4 right-hander with a mid-90s fastball and a forkball that dropped off the table. Americans got their first real look at him in March 1999, when the Baltimore Orioles played an exhibition in Havana. Contreras came out of the bullpen and threw eight scoreless innings, striking out 10 major league hitters. Scouts started making phone calls that night.

In 2000, he pitched for Cuba at the Sydney Olympics, where it won the silver medal. By then, every big-league organization had a file on him. None of them could do anything about it.

Then baseball in America fell apart. In the winter of 2001, during the chaos of the strike, the Cuban national team crossed into Mexico for a series of exhibitions against clubs in the suddenly booming Mexican winter league.

One of those games was in Monterrey, against the Sultanes, the team that had just added Craig Biggio and Jeff Bagwell. Contreras pitched well. That night, according to two people familiar with what happened, he walked out of the team hotel with a small bag and didn’t come back.

He’s never discussed the details publicly, and he didn’t on Saturday. “It was the hardest night of my life,” he said through an interpreter. “That’s all I’ll say.”

He spent the next several months in Mexico, establishing residency so he could sign as a free agent. When the new league formed, the bidding started. The Yankees, by most accounts, offered the most money. The Braves, who had just lost Greg Maddux and Tom Glavine and needed a starter badly, offered something else: Bobby Cox flew to Mexico himself.

“Bobby sat with him for three hours,” one person close to Contreras said. “He talked about family. He talked about patience. He promised him the ball every fifth day, no matter what. Jose had heard a lot of promises by then. That’s the one he believed.”

The Braves signed him for a signing bonus reported at more than $6 million. His salary, like every newcomer’s under the league’s rules, is near the minimum.

What happened

The easy version of this story is that Contreras has been a disaster. He’s 1-6, his ERA is 7.08, and he’s allowed 60 hits and nine home runs in 40⅔ innings. On a team that’s 14-27, last in MLB’s East Division and the most likely club in the league to be relegated, he’s been one of the main culprits.

The true version is more complicated.

Contreras has made eight starts. In three of them, against Miami on April 3, at St. Louis on April 29 and at Chicago on Saturday, he gave up 20 earned runs in 12⅓ innings. In the other five, he has a 3.81 ERA, including two quality starts.

The best of those came April 8, against the Yankees, the team that had wanted him most. He threw 6⅔ innings, allowed two runs and lost 2-0.

“When he’s right, he’s an ace,” one NL scout who has seen him three times said. “That start against New York, that was a No. 1 starter. The fastball was 95, the split was falling off the table, and they couldn’t touch him. But he’s not right very often. And when he’s wrong, he’s really wrong.”

What’s wrong? Ask five people and you’ll get five answers.

The scout thinks he’s tipping his forkball. “His glove does something different when he throws it. Good teams have noticed.”

Braves pitching coach Gary Sivertsen thinks it’s the schedule. “In Cuba they pitch on different rest, with a different ball, in a much shorter season,” he said. “Now he’s going every fifth day, travel days, time zones, big crowds. His body’s adjusting. It takes time.”

An AL executive who scouted him extensively in Cuba thinks it’s simpler than either. “He’s 30,” the executive said. “He’s in a new country. He doesn’t speak the language. His family is 90 miles from Florida and might as well be on the moon. Of course he’s not himself.”

The clubhouse

There’s a small group of people in the Braves’ clubhouse trying to help.

Gil, the 26-year-old catcher from Oaxaca, has become Contreras’s closest friend on the team, partly because they share a language and partly, Gil says, because “he’s the hardest-working guy I’ve ever caught. He doesn’t deserve what’s happening.”

Jose Rijo, who’s 37, spends a lot of time with him too. Rijo is a former World Series MVP who spent five years out of baseball after a string of elbow operations and came back last season against long odds. Now he has a 2.39 ERA out of the Atlanta bullpen.

“I tell him everybody gets lost,” Rijo said. “I was lost for five years. You find your way back. You just have to keep showing up.”

The other newcomer in the clubhouse is Hideki Matsui, the Japanese slugger, who’s on a one-year deal and hitting .252 with two home runs, a long way from what he did in Tokyo. Matsui and Contreras don’t share a language, but people around the team say they’ve developed a kind of quiet understanding. They eat lunch at the same table most days, and neither of them says much.

“Two guys a long way from home, both trying to prove something,” Gil said. “They get each other.”

The phone

The hardest part, people close to Contreras say, has nothing to do with baseball.

His wife and daughters are still in Cuba. Calls are difficult to arrange and expensive, and they can be cut off without warning. Some weeks he gets through. Some weeks he doesn’t. Several people independently say his worst starts have tended to come in the weeks when he couldn’t reach home.

Contreras won’t talk about his family on the record. When he was asked Saturday how he deals with being away from them, he looked at the photograph in his locker for a while before answering.

“I pitch for them,” he finally said. “Every pitch. Some days that makes it easier. Some days harder.”

Patience

The Braves are running out of room. They have a 57.5 percent chance of relegation, according to the league’s own projections. Mark Prior, their 21-year-old ace, has a 2.48 ERA and a 3-6 record, which says plenty about how much help he’s getting. The rotation behind him has been rough, and Contreras is a big part of why.

In most organizations, a pitcher with a 7.08 ERA on a team fighting relegation would already be in the bullpen, or in the minors, or gone. Cox hasn’t considered it, and he says so in a tone that ends the conversation.

“I made that man a promise,” Cox said. “He gets the ball every fifth day. That’s not changing. If we go down, we go down. But I’m not going to be the guy who tells Jose Contreras he can’t trust anybody in this country either.”

Contreras’s next turn comes this week in Houston, against Sammy Sosa, Alex Rodriguez and Jeff Kent, the most expensive lineup in the West. If he’s right, he can beat them. If he isn’t, it could be ugly.

On Saturday night, after everybody else had left the clubhouse at Wrigley, Contreras finally stood up. He folded his towel, put on his jacket and reached up to touch the corner of the photograph, the way someone might touch a doorframe on the way out of a house.

Then he turned off the light over his locker and went to catch the bus.
