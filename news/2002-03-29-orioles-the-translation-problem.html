ABL · 2002-03-29 · By Colin Mercer, Ball Theory

Orioles 2002: The Translation Problem

Baltimore's 2002 season comes down to two questions: what last year's numbers actually mean, and whether a 24-year-old right-hander is about to become the best pitcher in the ABL. We take them in that order.

Here’s the projection, so you can stop scrolling: 79-72. That’s about 715 runs scored, 685 allowed and a Pythagorean record a shade above .500. In a conference where the top three make the playoffs and the bottom three can be relegated, that makes Baltimore a solid but unspectacular contender for a playoff spot. It’s in the mix for promotion if things break right, and in no real danger of going down.

That’s the number. The interesting part is how much I don’t trust it, and why.

The translation problem

Every projection this spring has the same flaw, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. The 2001 statistics we’re all working from didn’t come from one league. They came from at least five, of wildly different quality, played in wildly different parks, against rosters assembled in the chaos after the court ruling.

A .300 hitter in the Mountain league, playing half his games at altitude against pitchers who were in Double-A two years ago, isn’t a .300 hitter. A 3.00 ERA in the California league, which had more established big leaguers than any regional circuit, might be worth more than a 3.00 ERA anywhere else. Our colleague Ed Marsh has spent the winter building translations across the regional leagues, the same way we translate minor-league numbers to major-league equivalents, and they’re our best guess. I’d put the error bars on them at roughly twice what we’d normally accept.

Baltimore is a perfect test case, because its roster comes from everywhere.

Start with Juan Uribe, the 23-year-old second baseman. Last year he hit .300/.325/.524 in 283 plate appearances, with 11 triples. That’s an .849 OPS, and he’ll probably be described this spring as an emerging offensive star. He played his home games in Denver, though, and he drew eight walks all season. Run that line through a park and league adjustment and you get something closer to .262/.292/.425: a useful player with a good glove and power for a middle infielder, and a hitter who will make an awful lot of outs.

Compare Frank Catalanotto. He hit .330/.391/.490 in 2001, in a hitter-friendly park, and the translation barely touches him. His walk and strikeout rates were good, his line drives were real, and the doubles power travels. Catalanotto is the closest thing this lineup has to a sure thing.

The lineup

Here’s what we expect, translated to an ABL context:

Frank Catalanotto 28 .300/.370/.455
Benny Agbayani 30 .275/.360/.420
John Vander Wal 36 .265/.360/.450
Carlos Lee 25 .275/.330/.460
Juan Uribe 23 .262/.292/.425
Xavier Nady 23 .260/.320/.430
A.J. Hinch 27 .225/.295/.360
Darren Lewis 34 .255/.310/.330
Rey Ordoñez 30 .240/.290/.320

A few observations:

This is an on-base team without much power. Catalanotto, Agbayani and Vander Wal all project to get on base 36-37 percent of the time, which is the foundation of a decent offense. But nobody here projects for 25 home runs. That matters at Camden Yards, which has played as a home run park for most of its life and generally rewards teams that can hit the ball over the fence.

Vander Wal is the age risk. He hit .299/.410/.562 in 2000, a genuinely great season, then fell off to a combined .270/.364/.444 in 2001. He’s 36. History says players his age with his profile lose something every year. Our projection assumes a modest decline. If it’s a steep one, this lineup loses one of its three on-base sources.

The middle of the field is a hole. Ordoñez is the most extreme player on the roster. He’s one of the best defensive shortstops alive, with three Gold Gloves, and he hit .247/.299/.336 last year. Marsh’s fielding numbers suggest the glove saves something like 12 to 15 runs a season. Our projection says the bat costs 20 to 25. Put Lewis in center and Hinch behind the plate, and Baltimore has three regulars who project to hit like replacement-level players at three of the most important positions on the field.

Mike Hargrove, who spent his playing career being called “The Human Rain Delay” for how long he took between pitches, will presumably bat those three together at the bottom of the order. Expect a lot of quick innings.

Nady is the swing piece. He hit .302/.381/.527 with 26 homers in A-ball last year at 22, which is very good and also very far from the ABL. The translation knocks him down to roughly .260/.320/.430. Talent evaluators who’ve seen him think that’s conservative. If he hits like the scouts think he can, he’s the power bat this lineup is missing. If he hits like the projection says, he’s a slightly below-average first baseman.

The rotation, part one: Oswalt

Now the good part.

The single best value of the winter’s draft may have been one almost nobody noticed. Houston, busy spending enormous money on short-term deals for stars, left Roy Oswalt off its protected list. Baltimore took him and is paying him the league minimum of $700,000.

Here’s what Oswalt did last year: 141.2 innings, a 2.73 ERA, 144 strikeouts and 24 walks.

That’s a 6-to-1 strikeout-to-walk ratio and a strikeout rate just over nine per nine innings, at age 23. You can argue about league quality and translations all you like; it doesn’t matter. Strikeout and walk rates translate across leagues far better than ERA does, and those are the numbers of a genuine ace. Voros McCracken’s work on defense-independent pitching, published last year, has convinced most of us that strikeouts, walks and home runs tell you much more about a pitcher’s real ability than his ERA does. By those measures, Oswalt might already be the best pitcher in the ABL.

Our projection has him at a 3.15 ERA with roughly 190 strikeouts. If anything, I think that’s conservative. If he stays healthy, he’s a Cy Young contender, and the Orioles got him for nothing.

The rotation, part two: everyone else

After Oswalt, things get complicated.

John Thomson had a 4.04 ERA last year, mostly in Colorado. That’s actually good: adjusted for park, it’s closer to 3.50, and his walk rate (25 in 93.2 innings) is excellent. The concern is home runs. He allowed 15, and some of that was altitude, but not all of it. Camden Yards isn’t going to help.

Then come the kids, and this is where my real worry lives.

Noah Lowry is 21 and threw 25 innings in short-season ball last year. Sergio Mitre is 21 and threw 91. The current plan, as far as anyone can tell, is that both will be in the rotation from the start, which means asking them to throw something like 150 to 180 innings each.

We’ve spent the last several years studying what happens to young pitchers who throw too many pitches, too soon. Rany Jazayerli and Keith Woolner’s work on Pitcher Abuse Points suggests the risk is greatest for pitchers in their early 20s who get worked hard in high-pitch-count starts. Asking Lowry to go from 25 innings to 150 is about as large a jump as you’ll ever see for a pitcher that age. I’d want the Orioles to cap both kids at around 120 innings and to keep a close watch on their pitch counts. Whether they will, with a playoff spot at stake, is another question.

Eleazar Mora, a 30-year-old with two solid seasons in Triple-A, is the realistic fallback. Our projection treats him as a league-average innings eater. That isn’t exciting, but it might be necessary.

The bullpen

Every preview I’ve read this spring calls Baltimore’s bullpen a strength. I’m not so sure.

Guillermo Mota, the closer, had a 5.26 ERA last year. The optimists point out that much of that came from nine home runs in 49.2 innings, which is the sort of thing that tends to even out over time. That’s true, as far as it goes. But Mota also struck out only 31 batters in those innings and walked 18. That isn’t a closer’s profile. It’s barely a setup man’s. He throws very hard, and that’s worth something, but velocity isn’t the same thing as missing bats. Our projection has him at about a 4.40 ERA.

John Rocker is the opposite problem. He can miss bats; he struck out 79 in 66.2 innings last year. He also walked 41. In 2000 he walked 48 in 53 innings. When Rocker throws strikes, he’s one of the best relievers in baseball. When he doesn’t, he’s a walk machine. Our projection, which basically splits the difference, has him at 3.80.

Rich Garces is the steadiest of the bunch: three straight years of solid ratios, a 3.90 ERA last year and no obvious warning signs. Of the three, he’s the one I’d trust in the ninth inning.

What it adds up to

Put it together and you get a team with a good on-base core, a big hole in the middle of the field, an ace, a reliable No. 2, two kids who probably shouldn’t be throwing this many innings, and a bullpen with more volatility than its reputation suggests.

That’s roughly 715 runs scored and 685 allowed, which works out to the 79-72 above. In our simulations, that’s good for:

  • Playoffs: about 38 percent.
  • Promotion: about 14 percent, since it requires reaching the American Championship.
  • Relegation: about 9 percent. Relegation in the ABL is split by conference depending on where the promoted FBL clubs come from, so the number moves a lot depending on what happens a tier below.

For context, other early previews have Baltimore anywhere from 75-76 to fifth in the ABL, which tells you how much uncertainty there is when nobody fully trusts the 2001 numbers.

The bottom line

The Orioles are built like a big-market club that hasn’t quite decided what it wants to be: good enough to contend, flawed enough to miss. If the kids hold up, Nady hits, and Oswalt becomes what the numbers say he already is, this is a playoff team with a real chance to go up. If not, it’s a .500 club with a great young pitcher and a lot of questions.

I’d bet on the second scenario, mostly because I’d bet on almost every team in this league being messier than its projection. But I’d bet on Oswalt being great either way.

And if you want one number to watch this season, don’t watch the standings. Watch Noah Lowry’s innings count. If it’s past 120 by August, worry.
