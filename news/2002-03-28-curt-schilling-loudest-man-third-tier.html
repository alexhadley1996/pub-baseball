FBL · 2002-03-28 · By Marty Kessler, Diamond Daily

The Loudest Man in the Third Tier

Curt Schilling shook Jerry Reinsdorf's hand and promised to pitch for Charlotte. Then the plan fell apart, the offers poured in, and Schilling did the strangest thing anyone in baseball has done this winter: he kept his word.

CHARLOTTE, N.C. — It takes about 90 seconds to figure out that the best pitcher in the Federal Baseball League isn’t going to stop talking.

He has opinions on pitch counts. He has opinions on the new television contract and on whether the FBL baseball is wound tighter than the MLB baseball. (It isn’t, he says, but he has a theory about the seams.) He has opinions on relegation, promotion, Rupert Murdoch, the infield dirt at CBi Park, the Battle of the Bulge and the best way to raid a dungeon in EverQuest. He has an opinion on this sentence, probably, and if you asked, he’d share it.

“People say I talk too much,” Curt Schilling says, leaning back in a folding chair in the Charlotte Knights clubhouse with a laptop open on his knees. “And I say, name one thing I said that was wrong. Take your time. I’ll wait.”

He does not wait. He keeps talking.

Here’s the thing, though. For a man who has never had an unspoken thought, the most interesting thing Schilling did this winter was something he barely talks about at all. He stayed.

The handshake

To understand how a 35-year-old pitcher who went 22-6 with 293 strikeouts last season ends up opening 2002 in the third tier of professional baseball, you have to go back to the fall of 2000, when the Supreme Court blew up the sport and Jerry Reinsdorf saw an opening.

While everyone else was scrambling, Reinsdorf was building an empire. His White Sox would run the new Midwest league. His Triple-A club in Charlotte would go into Atlanta’s new Southeast League as a beachhead. If the old MLB never came back, Reinsdorf would own two chunks of whatever replaced it.

A beachhead needs a general, and Reinsdorf went after the loudest one he could find.

“Jerry called me in November,” Schilling says. “He said, ‘I’ll pay you, I’ll build the team around you, and I’ll give you a microphone.’ And I’m sitting there thinking, the first two are nice.”

He signed, and for one glorious summer the plan worked. Schilling was the best pitcher in the Southeast League by a distance, Charlotte drew crowds, and Reinsdorf looked like a genius.

Then came January.

When Murdoch’s people sorted 60 franchises into three tiers, the new league’s founders, by most accounts thoroughly fed up with Reinsdorf, left the White Sox out of the top division altogether. Reinsdorf responded by renaming his club the Black Sox, which tells you everything about how that meeting went. Charlotte, cut loose from the empire it was supposed to anchor, landed in the FBL.

Schilling’s phone started ringing the next morning. Several clubs called, including at least two in MLB, according to people familiar with the calls. One club offered him a multi-year deal worth more than twice what he makes in Charlotte.

He turned all of them down.

“I shook a man’s hand,” Schilling says, and for the first time all afternoon the speed goes out of his voice. “Jerry’s plan fell apart. That’s not Charlotte’s fault, and it’s not these guys’ fault.” He waves at the clubhouse. “I told these people I’d pitch here. So I’m pitching here.”

Reinsdorf, reached through a spokesman, declined to comment, which makes him the only person in this story who did.

The list

There’s a second reason Schilling isn’t pitching in the big leagues this year, and it lives in Philadelphia.

Schilling spent most of the ’90s as a Phillie. He was the MVP of the 1993 NLCS, threw a five-hit shutout in Game 5 of that World Series, and famously watched Mitch Williams close games with a towel over his head. When the strike hit, the Phillies held the line harder than anyone. It cost them every player they had, and the organization has never forgiven the ones who left.

There is, people around the team will tell you, a list. Pat Burrell’s name is near the top. Jimmy Rollins is right behind him. Schilling has heard he’s on it too.

“Top five,” he says, beaming. “I’d be insulted if I wasn’t. You know how hard you have to work to get on a list in Philadelphia? Those fans booed Santa Claus. Making their list is like getting into the Hall of Fame.”

The weight room

Then there’s Roger Clemens.

In 1991, when Schilling was a 24-year-old Astro with a big arm and no idea what to do with it, Clemens cornered him in the Astrodome weight room and spent the better part of an hour telling him he was wasting his career. Schilling has called it the most important conversation of his life.

Eleven years later, Clemens is in the FBL too. After his clubhouse fight with Barry Bonds, the Yankees chose Bonds, and Clemens ended up in Louisville. So the two most famous right-handers of their generation will spend 2002 pitching in the third tier, in the same league, on teams that will play each other.

“I called him when it happened,” Schilling says. “I said, ‘Roger, you remember that speech you gave me in Houston? I’d like to give it back to you now.’”

What did Clemens say?

“Something I can’t repeat in Diamond Daily.”

Useless Schilling information

Because I can’t help myself:

  • Schilling was born in Anchorage, Alaska, where his father was stationed in the Army. This year his team plays in a league with a franchise in Alaska. He has already asked whether he can pitch the first series there. “Homecoming,” he says. “I was there for about a year. They don’t know me. They will.”
  • His son is named Gehrig, after Lou. Schilling has raised money for ALS research for a decade.
  • He keeps a database of every hitter he has ever faced, pitch by pitch, on the laptop in his lap. It has a folder labeled “FBL.” It’s already bigger than the one labeled “Southeast League.”
  • His catcher this year, Eddie Perez, spent most of the late ’90s as Greg Maddux’s personal catcher in Atlanta. Asked about the difference, Perez said, “Greg never said one word to me in five years. Curt has said about a million words since February.” He thought about it. “Both of them are right about everything.”
  • Charlotte’s left fielder, Mark Whiten, is one of only two men in history to drive in 12 runs in a game. Schilling has asked him to tell the story at least once a week since camp opened. “It never gets old,” Schilling says. “It’s gotten a little longer.”

The math

Can Charlotte actually go anywhere?

The rotation is legitimately deep for this level, with Schilling, Eric Milton and Gil Meche, and the preseason consensus ranks it among the best in the FBL. The lineup is another story. Edgardo Alfonzo is the one proven bat, Travis Fryman is 33, and one rival executive summed up the rest of the offense in four words: “Grady Sizemore or bust.”

Sizemore is 19. He turned down a football scholarship to the University of Washington to play baseball. Schilling has adopted him like a stray, which mostly means sitting next to him on every bus ride and explaining the Normandy invasion.

“He’s going to be a star,” Schilling says. “He just doesn’t know it yet. I keep telling him.”

The FBL sends two clubs straight up to the ABL and a third through a playoff. The preseason projections have Charlotte somewhere in the middle of the pack. Schilling has done the math his own way.

“Two promotions,” he says. “This year, then next year. We’re in MLB by 2004, I win a World Series with Charlotte, and then I retire and run for something.” He catches himself. “Not really. Probably not. Don’t write that.”

Sorry, Curt.

Will call

Every time Schilling pitches, he leaves a ticket at the will-call window for his father, Cliff, a career Army man who died in 1988, before his son ever threw a pitch in the big leagues. He has done it in Baltimore, Houston, Philadelphia and Charlotte. He’ll do it Sunday on Opening Day.

“Somebody asked me last year whether it bothered me that my dad’s ticket was in the Southeast League instead of the World Series,” Schilling says. “And I said, he never once in his life cared what league I was in. He cared whether I did what I said I’d do.”

He closes the laptop.

“So I’m doing what I said I’d do.”

Then he opens it right back up, because there’s a hitter on the Sacramento Solons he’s been meaning to look at, and an EverQuest raid at 11, and a reporter is still standing there, which means there’s still someone to talk to.
