FBL · 2002-03-29 · By Ken Albrecht, The Baseball Courier

Ichiro Wants Out of Orlando Before He's Played a Game

Sources say the Juicers sold Ichiro on a second-tier club, then helped it land in the third. Now his camp wants a trade, and Orlando isn't listening.

Ichiro Suzuki hasn’t played a game for the Orlando Juicers. According to four people with direct knowledge of the situation, he already wants out.

People close to the outfielder say he believes he was misled about where the Juicers would play this season, that the club has made marketing demands on him that go far beyond what he agreed to, and that he has no interest in spending his first season outside Japan as what one of them called “a theme park attraction.”

His representatives have asked Orlando’s front office about a trade at least twice this spring. Both times, those people say, the answer was no.

The Juicers deny that any trade request has been made. Ichiro, through an interpreter, declined to discuss it.

“I came here to play baseball,” he said Thursday after a workout. “That is all I want to talk about.”

The pitch

To understand how things went wrong this quickly, you have to go back to early January.

Ichiro, 28, was the most coveted player on the international market: seven straight batting titles in Japan’s Pacific League, a contact hitter with elite speed and arm strength, and a career .353 hitter for Orix. He was a free agent for the first time, and several clubs in the new league bid for him.

Orlando’s offer was the richest. According to two people familiar with the negotiations, it also came with an assurance that mattered to Ichiro more than the money.

At the time, the new league had not yet announced which of its 60 clubs would go into which tier. Orlando executives, those people say, told Ichiro’s representatives that the Juicers’ market, their resources and their owner — the Walt Disney Company, which created the franchise during the strike as an attraction at its Orlando resort — virtually guaranteed placement in the ABL, the second tier, and possibly higher.

Ichiro signed a one-year, $4 million contract. Two weeks later, the league announced the tiers. Orlando was in the FBL, the third.

What the Juicers knew

That alone might have been a misunderstanding. What happened behind it is the reason Ichiro’s camp is angry.

Under the new league’s revenue plan, clubs in the lower tiers receive a larger share of national television money for the first several years. The share shifts toward the top tier over a seven-year period. A lower-tier club also faces lower payroll expectations and lower travel costs.

Three people with knowledge of the sorting process told The Baseball Courier that Orlando’s ownership did not push for a higher placement. Two of them said the Juicers’ representatives actively argued for the FBL, citing the club’s limited playing history and the financial advantages of starting lower.

“They wanted the third tier,” one of those people said. “It was the most profitable place for them to be. They weren’t hiding it from the league.”

They may have been hiding it from Ichiro.

A Disney spokesperson said the company “participated fully and in good faith in the league’s placement process” and declined to comment on specific discussions. Juicers general manager Adam Nash said the club “never made any guarantee about placement to any player.”

The obligations

The second complaint is about what Ichiro has been asked to do off the field.

According to two people familiar with the arrangement, Ichiro’s contract includes a standard appearance clause. Orlando has interpreted it aggressively. This spring the club has scheduled him for autograph sessions at resort hotels, a photo shoot with costumed characters and a promotional video for Japanese travel agencies. Those agencies are now selling vacation packages that include tickets to Juicers games and, in at least one version, “a chance to meet Ichiro.”

Ichiro is famously private. In Japan, he kept the media at a distance and was known for a rigid daily routine, from his stretching to the way he cares for his bats. People close to him say the promotional schedule has cut into his preparation and that he finds the tour packages humiliating.

“He doesn’t mind signing for kids,” one of those people said. “He minds being sold.”

Juicers manager Tyler Allbritton, in his first year, chose his words carefully.

“Ichiro’s a professional,” he said. “He’s going to work hard and he’s going to play hard. Everything else is above my pay grade.”

The team

Ichiro also expected to win. The Juicers don’t look like a winner.

One preseason ranking put Orlando 18th of the 20 FBL clubs. It gave the pitching staff a D-minus and said the staff “is ugly outside of closer Byung-Hyun Kim.” The infield has real players in Alfonso Soriano, Sean Casey and 36-year-old Matt Williams. Beyond that, the lineup drops off fast. One widely circulated projection is kinder, at 77-74, but few people around the league expect the Juicers to contend for promotion.

That same preseason ranking named Ichiro one of three FBL MVP candidates. If he plays to his reputation, he may be the best hitter in the league on one of its worst teams.

The interest

At least two clubs have called Orlando about Ichiro this spring, according to people familiar with the conversations. One is in the ABL. The other is the Seattle Mariners, rebuilt last summer as a test case for the new league’s television model and badly in need of a star.

Orlando’s answer, those people say, was immediate: Ichiro isn’t available.

That position is easy to understand from the Juicers’ side. The club’s attendance projections and its Japanese tourism partnerships are built around him. Orlando is the only club in the FBL with two genuine fan favorites, according to the league’s preseason personality report. One of them is Ichiro. Trading him would mean rebuilding the entire marketing plan.

Ichiro has one real piece of leverage: his contract runs out after this season. If the Juicers keep him all year, they’re very likely to lose him for nothing in November, and they will have spent the year with a player who doesn’t want to be there.

“Everybody knows how this ends,” one rival executive said. “The only question is whether Orlando gets something for him first.”

Monday

The Juicers open the season Monday in Savannah against Barry Zito, a pitcher who turned down bigger offers to stay with a team he loves. Ichiro will bat leadoff and play right field.

Asked Thursday whether he was looking forward to Opening Day, he answered without hesitating.

“Every day I play baseball, I am happy,” he said.

Then he walked to the batting cage, alone, past a giant banner of himself, and took 150 swings without saying another word.
