MLB · 2002-04-16 · By Colin Mercer, Ball Theory

Bruce Chen Picks a Good Night

Bruce Chen came into Tuesday with a 6.88 ERA. He left Dodger Stadium with two outs in the ninth, 11 strikeouts, one hit allowed and an 8-0 Marlins win. The only Dodgers hit was Kory DeHaan's two-out single in the sixth.

LOS ANGELES — There’s a stat my colleague Ed Marsh likes to cite about pitchers who start the season badly: their April ERA tells you almost nothing about their May. It’s a small sample. Weird things happen. A pitcher can have a 6.88 ERA in three starts and still be a perfectly good pitcher.

Bruce Chen had a 6.88 ERA after three starts. On Tuesday night at Dodger Stadium he pitched 8⅔ innings, gave up one hit, struck out 11 and beat the Dodgers 8-0. That’s the small sample talking. It’s also, as far as I can tell, a very good left-hander having a very good night.

“I wasn’t worried about the ERA,” Chen said. “I was a little worried about the ERA. Not a lot.”

How he did it

Chen doesn’t throw hard. His fastball is the worst pitch in his arsenal by the scouts’ grades, and he knows it. What he has is a curveball that’s genuinely good, a changeup that’s nearly as good, and a sinker and slider he mixes in so hitters can’t sit on anything.

On Tuesday the curve was the pitch. He struck out five of the first nine Dodgers, three of them on curveballs in the dirt, and retired the first 10 in a row before walking Jason Varitek in the fourth.

“Bruce was throwing the curve for strikes early and then burying it late,” Marlins catcher Brian Schneider said. “When he does both, nobody hits it. Nobody.”

The Dodgers, who had won five straight a week ago and lost three straight since, didn’t have much of a plan.

“He kept us off balance all night,” Dodgers manager Jim Tracy said. “We were looking for something to drive and he never gave it to us. Credit to him.”

The no-hitter

It lasted into the sixth. With two outs, Kory DeHaan, who’d struck out in the first, lined a 2-2 changeup into right-center for a single. There was a smattering of applause from the 32,872 at Dodger Stadium, which tells you how the night had gone for them.

“I left it up,” Chen said. “A changeup up is a batting-practice fastball. He did what you’re supposed to do with it.”

Chen struck out the next batter, Tomas Perez. Then he retired the next eight, too.

The offense

For a while it looked like Chen would need to be perfect. Mickey Callaway, who came in with a 1.21 ERA, was nearly as good. The only runs he allowed came in the third, when Bill Hall and Schneider hit back-to-back homers.

Then the Marlins blew it open in the ninth: three singles to load the bases off Hector Carrasco and Brandon Villafuerte, an RBI single from Carlos Febles, and a grand slam from Carl Crawford, the 20-year-old left fielder, who hadn’t homered all season. It was 8-0, and the only question was whether Chen would finish.

Two outs

He nearly did. Chen got Chris Saunders on a fly ball and struck out DeHaan for his 11th strikeout, and then, at 118 pitches, walked Perez on a full count. Manager Frank Duffy came out and took the ball.

The crowd booed. Some of the Marlins in the dugout booed, too, and Chen said later that he did, a little, under his breath.

“I wanted it,” he said. “Everybody wants it. But Frank’s right. I’m 24. I want to pitch in September. You don’t throw 130 pitches in April to finish a game you’ve already won.”

Teddy Rose came in, gave up a double to Varitek, and got Juan Gonzalez on a fly ball to end it.

The Marlins are 13-3. Chen is 2-2. His ERA is 4.56. In another three starts, if Ed’s right, it’ll tell us almost nothing at all.
