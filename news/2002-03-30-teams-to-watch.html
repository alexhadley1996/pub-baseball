Teams to Watch: Six 2002 Season Previews

Carolina, Hartford, Baltimore, Portland, Philadelphia and Miami: the six clubs worth following before a pitch is thrown.

Six clubs, three leagues, one question each. Can Carolina's lineup outhit its rotation? Can Hartford's kids grow up before Gary Sheffield gets old? Does Baltimore have the arms? Is Portland really the worst team in the ABL? Can Chris Hammond un-retire his way into Philadelphia's pennant race? And is Miami for real? These are the teams I will be following all season, and here is what I think of them before a pitch is thrown.

Carolina Disco Turkeys (FBL) — 2002 Season Preview

The Disco Turkeys enter 2002 as one of the early favorites in the FBL, and it starts with a lineup that looks legitimately scary for this level. Ken Griffey Jr. and Orlando Cabrera give Carolina veteran star power up the middle, while Bubba Trammell and Terrence Long project as an effective corner-outfield pairing that can do damage without needing perfect matchups. Behind the plate, the Mike Matheny / Mike Lieberthal tandem should provide steady defense and game-calling, even if neither is expected to be an elite bat in this context.

The biggest reason for optimism, though, is Carolina’s wild card: Pablo Sanchez, a Mexican rookie unearthed out of the winter-league ecosystem. Sanchez has the kind of profile scouts love to argue about: a compact, somewhat unorthodox build, but tremendous bat-to-ball skill with real power upside. If he adjusts quickly to FBL pitching, he isn’t just a fun story — he’s a potential difference-maker for both 2002 and beyond.

On the pitching side, Carolina has a bullpen that feels deeper than most: Brendan Donnelly (sinker-heavy), Josias Manzanillo (slider), plus Danny Patterson and Vladimir Núñez as experienced arms. Donnelly, in particular, has the look of a real late-inning weapon — a sinker specialist with the kind of uncomfortable, herky-jerky life that makes hitters beat the ball into the ground or miss it outright.

The rotation is shakier. John Burkett is the “maybe there’s still something in the tank” veteran, while Frank Castillo and Blake Stein bring experience but need to corral their control — especially Stein. The staff is also thin on left-handed options, which could become a matchup problem against teams built to stack lefty bats.

Verdict: Carolina has enough offense to dream on promotion, but the front office may need to reinforce the pitching staff in-season — likely by flipping veterans at the right moment and targeting a stabilizing starter. Given the age and quality of the lineup, it would be a rational “win-now” push.
Expected Record: 88–63

Hartford Yard Goats (FBL) — 2002 Season Preview

Hartford profiles as another strong offense / low-ceiling pitching club, similar to Carolina — but with less margin for error because the defense projects older and slower.

The rotation headliner is Joe Mays, coming off a career year built on a sinker/changeup approach. The concern is obvious: Mays doesn’t miss bats, and in front of a defense that may not range well, that combination can unravel quickly. Behind him, Hartford is hoping for a series of favorable bounces and regressions: Chuck Finley and Doug Davis need the hit-rate gods to be kind, while Bob File and Tim Spooneybarger are critical bullpen pieces.

A defining question for Hartford is how much the young talent can contribute right now. Spooneybarger is only 22 and flashes promise with a lively fastball and a slurve. Offensively, the “next wave” hinges on José Guillén and Adam LaRoche. Guillén is the classic post-hype bat: loud power, questionable discipline. LaRoche is very young (drafted in 1999) but offers real long-term hitting potential.

All of that youth will be asked to support an aging but still dangerous core: Gary Sheffield (an MVP-caliber bat when healthy and locked in), Ellis Burks as the old-man power threat, and steady big-league competence from Ray Durham and Randy Winn up the middle.

Verdict: If the kids pop early, Hartford can hang in the mix; if not, they’re likely to hover around .500 — and Sheffield/Burks may become midseason trade dominoes if the standings break the wrong way.
Prediction: 78–73

Baltimore Orioles (ABL) — 2002 Season Preview

Baltimore is one of the larger ABL markets, and they feel built like it: talented, uneven, and carrying the weight of expectations.

The rotation has a strong foundation with John Thomson and Roy Oswalt, but the depth is a real concern. Behind them is a pair of 21-year-olds: Noah Lowry (lefty changeup specialist) and Sergio Mitre (sinkerball type with limited immediate upside). That’s a lot of innings being asked from young arms — and it can go south fast.

The bullpen is more encouraging: John Rocker and Rich Garcés bring veteran stability, and Guillermo Mota looks like the real thing with a high-octane fastball capable of shortening games.

The lineup is comparatively stable and should keep them competitive: John Vander Wal, Carlos Lee, Juan Uribe, and Frank Catalanotto form a solid, professional core — enough on-base skill and power to prevent long scoring droughts. A.J. Hinch will carry a heavy catching workload, and Xavier Nady could be a sneaky important piece as a right-handed bat if he develops quickly at age 23.

Verdict: The Orioles may spend 2002 fighting their lack of pitching depth. If the young starters hold water, Baltimore can surprise; if not, they’ll need a midseason arm (or two), and it’s unclear whether the timing/capital makes sense for a true “win-now” move.
Prediction: 75–76

Portland Beavers (ABL) — 2002 Season Preview

Portland enters as the odds-on relegation pick, and it’s not hard to see why. They have limited offense and power, and that would be survivable if the pitching staff were average — but it might be the weakest staff in the league.

Their “ace,” Dave Williams, is a 23-year-old who spent last year bouncing around the minors. Veteran Steve Paris brings similar risk, coming off a season where his walk/strikeout profile screams volatility. The wild card is Salvador Arellano, a 19-year-old Mexican arm with a good fastball but shaky control — the kind of profile that can either grow up fast or get punished fast.

The one real strength is the bullpen. Eric Gagné and Troy Percival give them an actual back-end plan, and veterans like Toby Borland and Jim Brower can soak up innings without melting down every other night. Micah Bowie is the lone lefty, but he has real weapons (slider/curve combo) and should miss enough bats to matter.

Verdict: Portland probably still gets dragged toward relegation, but they may not be the worst team if the defense holds and the veteran lineup provides enough baseline competence. The big strategic question is whether they flip Gagné/Percival at peak value — though holding Gagné for a 2003 push could be defensible if they believe they can reload.
Prediction: 66–93

Philadelphia Phillies (MLB) — 2002 Season Preview

The Phillies’ season has an oddly dramatic hinge point: Chris Hammond’s comeback story. He retired about five years ago, and now he’s back — throwing soft stuff and junk with surprising effectiveness. It’s compelling, and also kind of terrifying if you’re trying to win games.

The rotation’s clearest upside play is Brad Penny, still just 23 and the only arm that looks like a true difference-maker. Behind him are younger pitchers with varying degrees of projection, but not much proven MLB stability.

Fortunately, the offense and defense should give them a floor. Derek Jeter, Mike Cameron, and Roberto Alomar provide a strong contact-and-athleticism spine — good at-bats, steady production, and real defensive value supporting the staff.

But the headline is Miguel Cabrera, the 18-year-old Venezuelan phenom who looks ready to erupt at any moment. If Cabrera hits immediately, it changes the entire shape of the Phillies’ season.

Verdict: The offense should keep them afloat, but the pitching is likely to be the limiting factor over a full season. If Hammond fades and the young arms wobble, Philadelphia probably drifts back toward .500 by late summer.
Prediction: 76–75

Miami Marlins (MLB) — 2002 Season Preview

The Marlins have the feel of a high-variance team with real upside — an expansion roster that somehow landed star-level middle-of-the-order threats. Jason Giambi and Cliff Floyd give them a loud offensive base: if both are healthy and rolling, Miami can post legitimately elite run production for long stretches.

From there, the question becomes whether the supporting cast can keep getting on base ahead of the sluggers, and whether the pitching staff can prevent the kind of losing streaks expansion teams usually suffer when the bullpen gets overworked.

Verdict: A flashy offense gives Miami a real chance to surprise immediately — the key is whether they can find enough reliable innings behind their top arms to avoid the “good lineup, bad record” trap.
