MLB · 2002-03-31 · By Hal Brennan, Diamond Daily

Easter Sunday at Dodger Stadium

Pedro Martinez's first game as a Dodger was a complete game with 12 strikeouts against the most expensive lineup in baseball. He struck out Alex Rodriguez three times, finished the ninth himself, and the Dodgers beat Houston 4-3 on Juan Gonzalez's homer.

LOS ANGELES — Pedro Martinez came out of the dugout at Dodger Stadium a little after 1 o’clock on Easter Sunday, and 55,869 people stood up. He jogged to the mound, looked at the crowd for a second longer than pitchers usually do, and then turned around and threw a fastball to Craig Biggio’s replacement at the top of the Houston order for strike one.

Two and a half hours later he was still out there. Pedro threw a complete game in his first start for the team that once decided he was too small, struck out 12 and beat the Houston Astros 4-3.

“I told Tek before the game, I want to finish this one,” Pedro said, meaning Jason Varitek, his catcher in Boston and now here. “He said, OK, then don’t throw 140 pitches. I threw 121. I listened, a little.”

The lineup

Houston spent the strike building the most expensive lineup in the sport, and on Sunday most of it came up empty against Pedro. Alex Rodriguez went 0-for-4 and struck out three times. Sammy Sosa struck out twice and walked once. Jeff Kent struck out twice.

“He’s the best I’ve seen,” Rodriguez said. “I saw him in the American League for years. He’s the same guy. It doesn’t matter what league.”

It wasn’t all clean. Jeff Cirillo hit a two-run homer in the first inning, and the Astros took a 3-2 lead in the fifth on Vance Wilson’s single. But Pedro got stronger as the afternoon went on, and nobody in a Houston uniform scored after the fifth.

The kids and the veteran

The Dodgers’ offense was the part of the roster everybody worried about in March, and on Opening Day it did just enough. Wilson Betemit, the 20-year-old shortstop who was in Double-A when the strike started, hit a two-run homer in the second and tripled later. Tomas Perez tied the game with a single in the fifth.

Then Juan Gonzalez, who came over from the wreckage in Cleveland, led off the sixth with a homer off Jon Lieber, and that was the game. Lieber pitched well too, eight innings and six strikeouts, and lost.

The ninth

Manager Jim Tracy let Pedro go out for the ninth, which the crowd had been demanding since the seventh. A-Rod flied out. Sosa walked. Ron Gant singled, and with two outs, runners were on the corners and Tony Clark, the old Astro who crossed the line during the strike, was at the plate.

Pedro struck him out.

“I wanted that one,” Pedro said, and then refused to explain why, other than to say that he and Clark “have history, and the history is mine now.”

Ramon Martinez watched from the stands behind the Dodgers’ dugout, the same seats he sat in during spring training. He was asked afterward what he thought.

“He’s still the little brother,” Ramon said. “But today the little brother was the best one in the building.”

The Dodgers are 1-0. The Astros are 0-1. Pedro, for the record, is 1-0, which in this league means less than it might, because the Dodgers still have to find 70-some more wins somewhere to stay out of the ABL. But on Easter, nobody in Los Angeles was thinking about relegation.
