FBL · 2002-03-30 · By Walter Haines, special to Diamond Daily

Sacramento and the Thing About Numbers

The Solons say they're all in on analytics. I wanted to know what that means, whether it's right, and whether a baseball team can believe its own numbers too much. The answers are yes, mostly and probably.

A solon is a lawmaker. The word comes from Solon of Athens, who wrote the laws of the city 2,600 years ago, and it was applied to Sacramento’s old Pacific Coast League team because Sacramento is where California makes its laws. I have always liked the name. It suggests a team that thinks it knows the rules better than everyone else.

The new Solons, revived in the California league during the strike and now in the FBL, have decided to live up to it. Their owner, Mike Pelosi, made his money writing logistics software for Central Valley farms, and he has told anyone who will listen that his team will be “all in on data.” That’s a phrase I have learned to be wary of, because it usually means a team has bought a computer and a few spreadsheets and is now making the same decisions it always made, only with charts. I went to Sacramento expecting to find that.

I didn’t find that. I found a team that is actually doing something, which is rarer and more interesting, and which may or may not work.

Old Sacramento, by the way, has a history with strange numbers. In 1974 the Solons played in a football stadium with a left-field fence about 233 feet from home plate, and they hit more than 300 home runs in a season. Everybody understood that the numbers were nonsense. Nobody could stop looking at them anyway. That is, more or less, the problem with numbers in general, and I want to keep it in mind as we go.

What the Solons believe

As best I can tell from a week of conversations with Pelosi, general manager Randy Ready and manager Steve Ward, the Solons operate on five principles. Some of them are mine, in the sense that I’ve written about them for 25 years. Some aren’t. I’ll take them one at a time.

1. A minor league record is real information, often better information than a short major league record.

This is the big one, and it’s the one I agree with most.

Consider Aubrey Huff, the Solons’ first baseman. Last year, in his first extended look in the majors, Huff hit .248/.288/.372 in 434 plate appearances. That is a bad line for a first baseman, and the conventional reading is that Huff can’t hit major league pitching.

But the year before, in Triple-A, Huff hit .316/.394/.566, with 36 doubles and 20 home runs. Bill James worked out a method, years ago, for translating minor league statistics into their major league equivalents, and when you run Huff’s 2000 season through it, you get something like .285/.345/.495. That’s a good hitter. So which is the real Huff, the one from 434 major league plate appearances or the one from 464 Triple-A plate appearances translated to the majors?

The Solons’ answer is that both are evidence, roughly equal in weight, and that Huff, at 25, is still at an age when hitters improve. Players generally reach their peak around 27. A 24-year-old who struggled in his first full season isn’t a failed hitter. He’s a hitter who is two years from his best.

“Everybody looked at last year,” Ready says. “We looked at the last three years and his birthday.”

I think they’re right. I’d be very surprised if Huff hits .248 again. I’d be mildly surprised if he hits under .290.

2. For pitchers, strikeouts and walks tell you more than ERA.

The Solons’ rotation is built almost entirely on this idea, and it’s the one where I’m more cautious.

Brandon Knight is the clearest example. Knight spent most of 2001 in Triple-A, where he threw 162 innings, struck out 173 and walked 45. That’s an outstanding strikeout-to-walk ratio, and the Solons made him their No. 1 starter on the strength of it. He also pitched 10⅔ innings in the majors last year and gave up 12 earned runs and five home runs. The Solons regard those 10⅔ innings as statistical noise. Statistically, they’re right.

Octavio Dotel, the closer, is the same story told the other way around. In 2000 he had a 5.40 ERA, largely because he allowed 26 home runs. In 2001 his ERA fell to 2.66, and he struck out 145 batters in 105 innings. The Solons look at the strikeouts and conclude that the 2001 version is the real Dotel and the home runs in 2000 were mostly bad luck. Kyle Lohse, a 23-year-old with a 5.68 major league ERA but excellent minor league peripherals, got the same treatment.

Here’s my reservation. The research on this question, much of it published by Voros McCracken last year, suggests that pitchers have much less control over hits on balls in play than we used to believe, and that strikeouts, walks and home runs are a better guide to future performance. I think that research is basically right, and it’s one of the most important ideas in baseball in the last 20 years.

But “basically right” isn’t the same as “always right,” and I worry a little that the Solons treat it as gospel. A strikeout pitcher can lose his strikeout pitch. A man with great Triple-A numbers can lose something in the jump to a higher level. Knight’s 173 strikeouts last year are real evidence. If they turn into 100 strikeouts this year, the Solons’ model will tell them he’s getting unlucky, and he may simply have gotten worse. A model can’t tell you which, at least not early, and the Solons will have to decide what to believe.

3. Don’t give away outs.

Steve Ward is 37 and has never managed above Double-A. He also almost never bunts.

“An out is the only thing in baseball you can’t get back,” Ward says. “You get 27. Why would I give one away to move a guy 90 feet?”

This is correct, at least most of the time. The sacrifice bunt, in the great majority of situations, reduces the number of runs a team can expect to score. It increases the chance of scoring exactly one run, which matters late in a close game, and almost nowhere else. Ward knows that, and his rule is more like “rarely” than “never.”

I’ll add one thing he didn’t say. A manager who never bunts will be second-guessed every time his team loses a one-run game. Ward will be second-guessed a lot. He’ll have to be stubborn about this, and I think he’s stubborn enough.

4. Buy players before their prime, not during it.

The Solons like players in their mid-20s, and they’re openly skeptical of paying for players past 30. Huff is 25, Knight is 26 and Lohse is 23. Dontrelle Willis, a 20-year-old left-hander with a leg kick that looks as if it was designed by someone who had heard of pitching but never seen it, struck out 77 and walked 19 in short-season ball last year. He might be the best young pitcher in the FBL.

The Solons are not perfectly consistent about this, which I find reassuring. Kenny Lofton is 34. Terry Mulholland is 39. Benito Santiago is 37. Every team needs veterans, and these were affordable. But Lofton’s on-base percentage fell from .369 to .322 last year. The Solons are betting he’ll bounce back. I’d bet against it.

5. Use the whole roster.

The Solons carry specialists other teams wouldn’t bother with: Olmedo Saenz, who hits left-handed pitching very well and right-handed pitching not much at all, and Mike Myers, a sidearm left-hander whose whole job is getting one left-handed hitter out. The Solons will platoon more than any team in the FBL.

This is a sound idea that has been around for 80 years. Casey Stengel won 10 pennants with it. It doesn’t count as new, but plenty of good ideas aren’t new.

What the consensus says

The Solons are not, by consensus, a good team. One preseason ranking put them in the middle of the FBL, with matching C-minus grades for hitting and pitching, and described the lineup as having “enough competence, not enough fear.” The widely circulated projection has them at 75-76.

Here’s the problem with those numbers, from the Solons’ point of view: the projections are built on the very statistics the Solons have decided not to believe. The projection sees Huff’s .248, Knight’s 10.12 major league ERA and Lohse’s 5.68. The Solons see Huff’s Triple-A line, Knight’s strikeouts and Lohse’s peripherals.

So this season is a referendum, of a sort, on which set of numbers is telling the truth.

My own guess, for what it’s worth, is that the Solons will score about 720 runs and allow about 680. That works out to roughly 79 or 80 wins in a 151-game season. That’s better than the projections, not as good as the Solons hope, and very possibly wrong in either direction by 10 wins.

About Portland

Every story about a data-minded team in this league now has to mention Portland, where Jake Kirk is running a similar experiment two tiers up. The two clubs are often lumped together.

They’re actually quite different. Portland’s core idea is about when innings are pitched: put your best pitchers where the leverage is. Sacramento’s core idea is about which numbers to believe: minor league records over small major league samples, strikeouts over ERA, youth over reputation. Both are reasonable. Neither is proven. If both teams succeed, it will be a good year for people like me. If both fail, I’ll be writing a column in October explaining why it wasn’t the ideas’ fault.

Pelosi, for the record, says he has never met Kirk. “I’ve read about him,” he says. “He seems smart. I’d like to beat him.” Since they’re in different leagues, that would require one of them to change tiers, which is a sentence that could only be written about this particular universe.

The danger

I want to end with a caution, because I’ve been doing this a long time and I’ve seen what happens when teams fall in love with their own numbers.

The value of a statistic is that it tells you something your eyes can’t. The danger of a statistic is that it can persuade you to stop looking. A team that has decided in advance which numbers are real will be tempted to explain away every number that disagrees with it. It will call a bad month bad luck and a good month regression to the true talent, and it will be right often enough to keep believing, and wrong often enough to lose.

The Solons are smarter than most teams, and I think they’ll be better than their projections. But they’ll learn things this year that their models can’t tell them yet. I hope they’re willing to.

They open Monday at home against Albuquerque, with Knight on the mound. The Solons will be watching his strikeouts. Everybody else will be watching his ERA.

If those two numbers start to disagree, and someday they will, that will be the most interesting thing that happens in Sacramento this year.
