MLB · 2002-03-26 · By Owen Kirby, Diamond Daily

The Big Unit, Behind the Lens

Randy Johnson walked away from baseball for a year to take pictures of other people. Then the Red Sox found him on a frozen sideline in Foxboro.

FORT MYERS, Fla. — The first thing Randy Johnson did when I walked up to his locker at City of Palms Park was take my picture.

He didn’t ask. He just lifted the Nikon off the shelf, aimed it down at me (and from 6-foot-10, believe me, it’s down), and fired three frames before I’d said a word.

“Hold still,” he said.

“I’m holding still.”

“You’re not in focus.”

“I’m short.”

“I noticed.”

He looked at the screen, frowned, and put the camera back on the shelf next to his glove. And that, for a while last year, was the whole point. For one season, the most intimidating pitcher on the planet stopped being the person everyone looked at and became the person doing the looking.

Walking away

Here’s what you need to know about Randy Johnson’s last two years, because they’ve been strange even by the standards of this strange new baseball world.

When the strike shut everything down in 2000, Johnson stayed out with the rest of the union. He went home to Phoenix, dug out the cameras he’d bought as a photojournalism student at USC, and started shooting: rodeos, high school football, a few rock concerts. “I needed something to do with my hands,” he says, “that wasn’t throwing at somebody.”

Then came the 2001 season, and a decision almost nobody saw coming. Arizona’s owners, flush with startup money from a group of investors, signed up for the new Southwest league. The thinking around the club, according to people who were there, was simple: thinner competition meant easy wins. They figured their ace would strike out 400 guys and they’d sell a lot of tickets doing it.

Johnson said no.

“They wanted me to go strike out guys who sell insurance in the offseason,” he says. “I’ve got nothing against insurance. It’s not baseball. I wasn’t going to pretend it was.”

So he took the year off. At 37, with four Cy Young-caliber seasons behind him, he picked up a media credential and spent 2001 working as a freelance sports photographer. He shot college football in Tempe, NHL games in Phoenix, and a heavy-metal festival in the desert where, he says, “nobody recognized me, because everybody there was tall and had long hair.”

He shot three Diamondbacks games from the photographers’ well, too. He hated all three.

“I’d see a pitch I would’ve thrown differently, and I’d have to just take a picture of it,” he says. “That was the worst part of the whole year. That was also when I knew I’d come back.”

The snow

The way he came back is the best part of the story, and it involves football.

By last fall Johnson was good enough, and famous enough, that an agency started sending him to NFL games. In January he got the assignment of a lifetime: the AFC divisional playoff, Raiders at Patriots, the last game ever played at old Foxboro Stadium. You remember it: the snow, the tuck rule, Adam Vinatieri’s 45-yard kick through the blizzard to tie it, and his 23-yarder to win it in overtime.

Johnson was in the end zone for all of it, under a parka, with a 400-millimeter lens.

So was a Red Sox executive who, it should be noted, had no business being at a football game three weeks before the most important draft in his franchise’s history. He asked that his name be left out of this story. “I’m not supposed to have been there,” he said. “Everyone in the office thinks I was at a scouting meeting.”

He spotted Johnson in the second quarter. “There’s a seven-foot man in the end zone with a camera, in a snowstorm,” he says. “It isn’t hard.”

At halftime he worked his way down to the field level and asked Johnson a single question: was he done with baseball?

“And Randy looks at me, with snow all over his eyebrows,” the executive says, “and says, ‘Who’s asking?’”

Boston was asking. The Red Sox had spent two stubborn years insisting they were still the only real major league team left. They’d just been handed a place in the new league’s top tier and, at almost the same moment, had lost Pedro Martinez to Los Angeles. They needed an ace badly. They also had the one pitch they knew Johnson would listen to: this time, the top tier would be real.

“He said every other team was selling me something,” Johnson says. “Boston was the only team that said it was going to be hard. Hard was what I wanted.”

Arizona, assuming its ace was retired, left him off its protected list when the league held its draft in late January. Boston took him, and a week later the two sides agreed to a three-year deal. Johnson’s first act as a member of the Red Sox was to fly to New Orleans and photograph Super Bowl XXXVI, which he’d already been booked for.

He was on the sideline when Vinatieri’s 48-yarder beat the Rams as time expired. He has the frame on his laptop: the ball in the air, the uprights, a second-year quarterback named Tom Brady with his arms half raised.

“Two weeks, two Vinatieri kicks, and I’m a Red Sox,” he says. “New England’s a weird place.”

Still the Unit

Here’s the question everyone is asking in Fort Myers: what does a 38-year-old pitcher look like after two years away?

If this spring is any indication, he looks exactly the same.

“The fastball’s 97, and the slider still does that thing where it starts at your hip and ends up in the dugout,” one American League scout said. “Most guys his age have 400 extra innings on their arm. He’s got 400 fewer. He might be better rested than he’s been since college.”

The rest is still there too: the glare, the impatience with questions he considers stupid (which is most of them), and the habit of staring in at the hitter for so long that you start to wonder if he’s forgotten the hitter is there. A few things the young Red Sox have learned:

  • The nickname: he got “Big Unit” from Tim Raines, his Montreal teammate, after the two collided in batting practice in 1988. Raines is 42 and playing for Detroit, which is in Boston’s division. “I’ll say hello,” Johnson says. Then: “After.”
  • The trades: Seattle got him from Montreal in 1989 for Mark Langston. Then the Mariners traded him to Houston in 1998 for Freddy Garcia, Carlos Guillen and John Halama. Garcia is in Oakland now and Halama is in Toronto, which is also in Boston’s division. Johnson was asked whether he follows the careers of the players traded for him. “No,” he said. Pause. “Garcia’s doing fine.”
  • The famous at-bat: in the 1993 All-Star Game, he threw his first pitch to John Kruk over Kruk’s head. Kruk spent the rest of the at-bat bailing out toward the dugout and struck out. “Everybody asks about that,” Johnson says. “Kruk was smart.”
  • The photo habit: he has taken more pictures this spring than every newspaper in Florida combined. His favorite subject is Edwin Jackson, an 18-year-old right-hander born on an Army base in Germany who might be in the rotation by summer. “He’s got a great face for it,” Johnson says. “Nothing’s happened to it yet.”

His catcher, 26-year-old Toby Hall, has been warned about the glare. “He calls his own game,” Hall says. “He also told me where to stand for the team picture. I’m pretty sure he was right about both.”

Easter Sunday

The preseason consensus doesn’t love Boston. The lineup is solid, with Rich Aurilia, Fred McGriff, Pat Burrell, the kid Chone Figgins and a lot of patient hitters. But behind Johnson the rotation is Pedro Astacio, Colby Lewis, Brian Sikorski and hope. One rival executive put it bluntly: “Randy’s going to win 20 games and they’re going to finish .500, and he’s going to look at everybody in that clubhouse like it’s their fault.”

Manager Grady Little has already named Johnson the Opening Day starter. It’s Sunday, at Fenway, on Easter, against the Yankees.

Which means Barry Bonds.

Johnson has faced Bonds plenty. His last pitch in a game that counted came in October 1999, but he spent a lot of the late ’90s pitching against Bonds in the NL West. He’s also photographed him: last summer he shot a Yankees exhibition from the third-base well.

“Through a 400-millimeter lens, he looks exactly like he does from 60 feet, 6 inches,” Johnson says. “Big, and patient, and waiting for you to make a mistake.”

So what’s the plan?

“Don’t make a mistake.”

At the end of the day, as the clubhouse empties out, Johnson pulls the Nikon back off the shelf and walks out to the bullpen alone. The light’s going orange over the outfield wall, and he shoots it for 10 minutes without saying a word. Then he packs the camera into its case, sets it gently on the top shelf of his locker, and picks up his glove.

“That’s for later,” he says, nodding at the camera. “This is for now.”

I asked if he’d shoot Fenway on Sunday.

“Somebody else can,” he said. “I’ll be busy.”
