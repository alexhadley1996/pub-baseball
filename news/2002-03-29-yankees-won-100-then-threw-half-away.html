MLB · 2002-03-29 · By Rob Lindqvist, Diamond Daily

The Yankees Won 100 Games. Then They Threw Half the Team Away.

How the league's big bad guys jumped first, won everything and then tore it down on purpose, and why the plan is either brilliant or the most arrogant thing a baseball team has ever done.

TAMPA, Fla. — There’s a trophy in George Steinbrenner’s office at Legends Field that didn’t exist two years ago. It’s silver, about the size of a fire hydrant, and engraved “KING OF NEW YORK.” The Yankees won it last October by sweeping the New York Knights in four games by a combined score of 39-5.

Steinbrenner likes to touch it when he talks.

“People ask me why we changed things,” he says, hand resting on the trophy. “We won 100 games. Why change? I’ll tell you why. Because I want to win 100 games this year, and next year, and every year after that. The Yankees don’t keep stars. We make them.”

That’s either the smartest thing anyone in baseball has said this winter or the most dangerous. I’ve spent a week in Tampa trying to figure out which, and I’m still not sure.

Act I: Jumping first

To understand this roster, you have to understand that the Yankees have been playing a different game from everyone else for two years.

When the players walked out before the 2000 season, most owners sat tight. The Yankees did not. In July 2000 they organized their own league, a four-team circuit with an independent club in Hoboken, a revived Brooklyn franchise, the newly independent Hartford Yard Goats and an expansion team in Queens called the Knights. They signed established players to short, enormous contracts and started selling tickets.

Major League Baseball responded by suing its own most valuable franchise. It wasn’t the first time the two had gone to court; Steinbrenner sued baseball over a sponsorship deal in 1997. This time it ended differently. In October 2000 the Supreme Court used the case to strike down baseball’s antitrust exemption entirely, and the old MLB collapsed in an afternoon.

“Technically, we lost that lawsuit,” Steinbrenner says. “Technically.”

Act II: The superteam

What followed was the most absurd season in Yankees history, which is saying something.

The 2001 Yankees had Greg Maddux, Roger Clemens and Barry Bonds. They played a 118-game schedule in a tri-state league against the Knights, Hoboken, Brooklyn and Hartford, and went 100-18. Bonds hit 79 home runs. The tabloids started calling them the Bronx Bad Boys, and the Yankees started selling T-shirts that said it.

I want to be careful here, because 100-18 is a ridiculous number and it deserves some context. That’s an .847 winning percentage. No team in the modern era has come close. But the opponents were mostly expansion clubs and independents, and Hoboken’s Opening Day starter last year had been selling cars eight months earlier. A good team would’ve won 85 of those games. The 2001 Yankees were more than a good team, but they weren’t the 1927 Yankees with better marketing either.

Then came January, and Rupert Murdoch’s plan: 60 clubs, three tiers, promotion and relegation, and a giant national TV contract. Some clubs balked. The Yankees signed almost immediately.

“Why wouldn’t we?” says Matt Troxel, the 41-year-old the Yankees hired in January to run the new operation. “We had a two-year head start on everybody. Every other team was trying to figure out how to build a roster. We already had one.”

So why did they blow it up?

Act III: The bonfire

This is the part that has the rest of baseball shaking its head.

Under the new league’s draft rules, each club could protect between 10 and 15 veterans, plus a group of players under 20. Anyone left unprotected was fair game. Most clubs that had stockpiled stars during the chaos protected as many as they could.

The Yankees protected Bonds and Maddux and then submitted a list that one rival executive called “the strangest piece of paper I’ve ever seen.” Left unprotected:

  • Derek Jeter, 27, the best young shortstop in the game. Philadelphia took him.
  • Mariano Rivera, the best reliever on earth. The A’s network took him.
  • Bernie Williams, also taken by the A’s network.
  • Jorge Posada, taken by Southside.
  • Mike Mussina, taken by San Juan.

Clemens wasn’t on either list by then. After his clubhouse fight with Bonds late last season, the Yankees picked Bonds and let Clemens go. He’ll open the season pitching for Louisville in the third tier, which is a sentence I still can’t quite believe I’m typing.

When I asked about Rivera specifically, Troxel offered the closest thing to a philosophy I heard all week. “Relievers are the most replaceable players in baseball,” he said. “That’s not a controversial statement anymore.”

I’ve written some version of that sentence in this space more than once. I’d never have applied it to Mariano Rivera.

So what’s the logic? There is one, and it’s more cynical than anything Steinbrenner said in public.

Look at the contracts. A veteran drafted by a new club can sign a long-term deal or take a one-year contract set by league arbitration. Jeter took the one-year deal in Philadelphia. So did Mussina in San Juan. Both will be free agents next winter, and the Yankees know exactly who will have the most money when they are.

The YES Network, the Yankees’ own TV channel, launched this month. That’s on top of the national money from Murdoch’s deal, which, for the first few years, deliberately sends a bigger share to the lower tiers. The Yankees accepted less national money in the short term because they already have more local money than anyone.

“Let me put it this way,” one AL executive said. “They didn’t throw those guys away. They lent them out. Next November they’ll buy back whoever they want, and they’ll have saved $40 million doing it.”

Troxel didn’t deny it. “Every team in this league is going to have to make hard decisions about money for the next seven years,” he said. “We made ours first. Like everything else.”

Act IV: The roster that’s left

Here’s what the Yankees actually have heading into Sunday.

One superstar. Bonds is 37 and coming off the greatest power season in baseball history, in a league where the pitchers were selling cars. He’s on a one-year deal, by his own choice. “Barry bets on Barry,” his agent likes to say.

One great pitcher. Maddux is 36, signed through 2006, and has been told he’s the only member of last year’s rotation the Yankees wanted. “I was told I’m one of the handful,” he says. “I’m still trying to figure out who else is in the hand.”

And then a very specific kind of roster. Torii Hunter in center field, signed after the Twins went out of business. Bengie Molina behind the plate. Mark Grudzielanek and Adam Kennedy in the middle infield. Kris Benson, coming back from elbow surgery, in the rotation. Chad Bradford, a submariner who throws about 82 miles per hour, in the bullpen with Jeff Tam, Cliff Politte and closer Mike DeJean.

If you squint, it looks like a roster Billy Beane would build: lots of good gloves, a deep bullpen of guys other teams didn’t value and very little money spent on anything that isn’t Bonds or Maddux. The preseason consensus agrees with me, mostly. It ranks the Yankees first in MLB, with the best rotation and deepest bullpen in the league.

“They took Oakland’s playbook,” the AL executive said, “and stapled a hundred million dollars to the front of it.”

The offense is the problem, and everybody knows it. Bonds aside, Hunter is the only regular with a projected league-average bat. The obvious question is the one rival managers are already asking out loud: why would anyone pitch to Bonds?

“Then they’ll walk him,” Joe Torre says, shrugging. “And Torii will hit. And Bengie will hit. And when they don’t, Greg Maddux will pitch. I’ve managed worse problems.”

Does Torre miss Jeter?

He waits a long time before answering.

“I’d be lying if I said I didn’t,” he says finally. “He was like a son to me. But that’s not my department. My department is Sunday.”

Act V: Everybody else

You don’t have to look hard to find people in this league who’d like to see the Yankees fail.

The Knights haven’t forgotten 39-5. The Gothams haven’t forgotten last season either; Brooklyn was so bad in the tri-state league that it had to be sold. The Phillies, a franchise that holds a grudge the way other franchises hold a lease, now employ the Yankees’ former shortstop.

Jeter, reached in Clearwater, was exactly as diplomatic as you’d expect.

“I’m a Phillie,” he said. “I’m focused on Philadelphia.”

Did he feel betrayed?

“I’m focused on Philadelphia.”

Would he consider going back to New York next winter?

“I’m focused on Philadelphia.” Then, almost smiling: “Ask me in November.”

The verdict

So which is it, brilliant or arrogant?

I think it’s both, which is very Yankees.

The theory is sound. Stars on one-year deals are rentals whether you like it or not, defense and bullpen depth are cheap and underrated, and in a league where the money tilts toward the top a little more each year, the team with its own TV network can rebuild through free agency faster than anyone. If Bonds stays healthy and Maddux is still Maddux, the Yankees should win the East.

But the whole plan rests on two players in their late 30s, and the Yankees have spent two years making enemies of nearly every club, executive and fan base in the league. If Bonds gets hurt, or teams walk him 200 times and nobody behind him can hit, the Yankees will be a very ordinary team that everyone else desperately wants to beat.

They open Sunday at Fenway against Randy Johnson, on Easter.

“Good,” Steinbrenner says when I mention it. “Start with the hardest one.”

Then he pats the trophy one more time and shows me out.
