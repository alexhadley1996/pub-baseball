MLB & FBL · 2002-03-29 · By Ned Calloway, Diamond Daily

Same School, Different Plans

Mark Prior and Barry Zito grew up on the same San Diego field and pitched for the same college. One of them wants to be an ace right now. The other one wants to have a good time and figures the ace part will sort itself out.

KISSIMMEE, Fla., and SAVANNAH, Ga. — Mark Prior has a plan. You can tell because he tells you.

“I want the ball on Opening Day,” he says, sitting at a picnic table outside the Braves’ spring complex. “I want 33 starts. I want to be the guy they hand the ball to when the season’s on the line. Not in three years. This year.”

He’s 21. He hasn’t thrown a professional pitch. He says all of this calmly, the way you’d tell someone your height.

Four hundred miles north, in a dugout in Savannah, Barry Zito is lying flat on his back on the bench with his eyes closed. A teammate tells him a reporter is here. He doesn’t open his eyes.

“Do I have a plan?” he repeats. “Man, I had a plan once. It was ‘drive to Georgia.’ It’s worked out great.”

These two grew up a couple of years apart at the same San Diego high school. Both ended up pitching for USC, a year apart, close enough that they kept hearing about each other and never quite overlapped. Both are, by any reasonable scouting measure, among the best young pitchers alive. They have about as much in common as a slide rule and a lava lamp.

The plan

Start with Prior, because he’d want to go first.

The Yankees drafted him out of high school in 1998, and he turned them down to go to college: Vanderbilt first, then USC. Last spring he went 15-1 with a 1.69 ERA and 202 strikeouts, won the Golden Spikes Award as the best amateur player in the country, and was supposed to be the second pick in the June draft.

Except there wasn’t a June draft. The old system was in pieces, and nobody had the authority to hold one. So the most polished college pitcher in a generation walked off the USC campus as a free agent, and every team with money called.

The Yankees called again. So did Houston, San Francisco and a couple of clubs that would rather not be named. Under the new league’s rules, the salary was the same everywhere: like every new professional, Prior will make the league minimum, $500,000, until he’s eligible for arbitration. What teams could compete on was the signing bonus, and most of them bid big. Most of them also offered, in one form or another, an apprenticeship: a year in the minors, a few spot starts, maybe a September call-up.

Atlanta offered something else.

“They’d just lost Greg Maddux and Tom Glavine,” Prior says. “Two of the best pitchers who ever lived, gone in one winter. I looked at that and thought, there’s a hole there. Somebody has to fill it.”

So he signed with the Braves for a bonus reportedly less than half of what the Yankees put on the table. The Braves’ entire pitch, as Prior tells it, fit into one sentence: if he was as good as everyone said, he’d start Opening Day.

“Money’s the same in five years,” Prior says. “Opening Day isn’t.”

Bobby Cox has made it official: Prior pitches Sunday in Detroit. Cox has managed some very good young pitchers in his life, and he says none of them did what Prior did in December.

“He called me and asked for the ball,” Cox says. “In December. I’ve had guys ask for the ball in the seventh inning. I’ve never had a guy ask in December.”

The scouts love him, and what they love first is the delivery, which they keep calling textbook. The arm action is clean, the stride is long, and everything moves in the same direction at the same time. One NL scout said watching Prior throw is “like watching a guy who learned to pitch from the instruction manual and then made the manual better.”

But nearly every scout who praises Prior adds a “but,” and it has nothing to do with him.

The Braves are not good. Prior is surrounded by kids and question marks: Steve Finley, at 37, is the most reliable hitter in the lineup, and the bullpen is young and unproven. The preseason projections have Atlanta near the bottom of MLB, and the bottom of each division goes down to the ABL.

“Here’s what worries me,” one AL scout said. “Bad teams ask their best young pitcher to win games by himself. That means 120, 125 pitches because nobody in the bullpen can be trusted. Prior’s going to say yes every time. That’s who he is. Somebody else has to be the one who says no.”

Prior has heard that. He doesn’t love it.

“I’m not fragile,” he says. “I take care of myself. I know my body. If they want me to throw 120, I’ll throw 120.” He thinks about it. “I’d like to throw 120 because we’re winning, though.”

The vibe

Now Zito, who would happily go second, or third, or not at all if the line were long.

In the spring of 2000, he was 21, Oakland’s first-round pick from the previous June, sitting in an A’s system that had stopped playing games because of the strike. He spent his days surfing and playing guitar. One night he saw a local news clip about a summer team in Georgia called the Savannah Bananas: players dancing between innings, a pep band, a sold-out crowd losing its mind over a bunt.

“And I thought, those people are having more fun than anyone in baseball,” Zito says. “I want to be where the fun is.”

He loaded his car and drove across the country that week. He has been a Banana ever since.

In 2001 the Bananas joined Atlanta’s new Southern League, which means Zito spent last summer pitching against the Braves, who were not amused by the dance routines. People familiar with the negotiations say Atlanta tried to buy him from Savannah twice. Both times, Zito said he was happy where he was. Both times he meant it.

When the new league sorted itself out in January, Savannah landed in the FBL, the third tier, and protected Zito with its first pick. The bigger offers came again. He turned those down too.

“People keep asking when I’m going to leave and go be an ace somewhere,” he says. “And I’m like, I’m an ace here. They hand me the ball, the Banana Nanas dance, I throw a curveball, and 4,000 people scream like I invented baseball. What’s the better version of that?”

(For the uninitiated: the Banana Nanas are a dance team made up entirely of grandmothers. They are not a gimmick. Or rather, they’re a gimmick, and they’re also extremely good.)

What do the scouts make of all this? Mostly they shake their heads. Zito’s curveball drops off the table, a true 12-to-6, and one scout called it “the best curve in professional baseball at any level, and I’ve seen it in front of a guy in a banana suit doing the worm.”

He’s 24, and nobody is quite sure how good he’d be in MLB. Zito doesn’t seem worried about it.

“I’ll be an ace when the universe says I’m an ace,” he says. “Right now the universe says I’m a Banana.”

His manager, Brett Merriman, has learned not to fight it. “Barry does yoga before he pitches, he meditates, he plays guitar on the bus, and I let him,” Merriman says. “Because every fifth day he goes out there and gets people out. I’ve got a closer who hasn’t washed his cap since the Clinton administration. Barry’s the normal one.”

That closer, Steve Kline, overheard this and confirmed it. “Second Clinton administration,” he said. “Get it right.”

Zito’s rotation-mate, Hideo Nomo, was asked what he thinks of the Bananas’ between-innings dancing. Nomo considered the question for a long time.

“I watch,” he said.

About each other

Neither of them could believe the other had been asked about him.

“Mark had a five-year plan in high school,” Zito says. “Like, a real one. Written down. I had a five-minute plan. Most days it was lunch.”

He laughs, then gets a little more serious.

“No, I love that guy. He’s the most focused person I’ve ever met. In high school he ate lunch like he was being graded. He’s going to be great. I hope Atlanta lets him be great for a long time, you know? Not just this year.”

Prior, told about the lunch line, almost smiled.

“Barry was the most talented guy who ever came through that school,” he says. “I don’t understand him. I’ve never understood him. But I’ve never seen anybody throw a curveball like that, and I’ve watched a lot of curveballs.” He pauses. “He’s wasting it in Savannah. And he’d tell you he isn’t, and he’d mean it, and I’d still think he was wrong.”

Opening Day

The schedule-makers, by accident, have given them back-to-back Opening Days.

Prior pitches Sunday in Detroit, his first professional start, in the top tier, against a lineup with Carlos Delgado, Troy Glaus and a 42-year-old Tim Raines. He’ll spend Saturday night going through scouting reports.

“I’ve already got them,” he says. “I’ve had them since last week.”

Zito opens the FBL season Monday at home against Orlando, which means his first pitch of 2002 might go to Ichiro, who has never seen a curveball like Zito’s. Zito will spend Sunday night on the beach at Tybee Island if it’s warm enough, and playing guitar on his porch if it isn’t.

“Ichiro, man,” he says, grinning. “I’ve heard he’s unbelievable. I can’t wait. That’s going to be so fun.”

Fun. Prior didn’t use the word once in two hours. Zito used it 31 times. I counted.

Both of them are going to be very good. Only one of them is in a hurry.
