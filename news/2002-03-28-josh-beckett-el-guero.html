FBL · 2002-03-28 · By Sam Holloway, Sporting Weekly

El Güero

Josh Beckett could have pitched for a lot of teams this year. He picked the one where the mariachis play him out of the bullpen.

SAN ANTONIO — Josh Beckett is 21 years old, throws 97 mph and has the general demeanor of a man who has never once been told no by anything, including gravity.

He walks into the San Antonio Rebeldes’ clubhouse wearing a camouflage hat, cowboy boots and a T-shirt that says “DON’T MESS WITH TEXAS,” which, if you’ve ever stood in a batter’s box against him, is less a slogan than a medical warning.

“Mornin’,” he says to the clubhouse attendant, in English. Then, to the bullpen catcher: “¿Qué onda?” Then, to me, with a grin that could sell pickup trucks: “You’re the guy from New York? Y’all are a long way from home.”

So is Beckett, sort of. And also, sort of, he isn’t. That’s the whole story.

How he got here

Quick version: in 1999, the Florida Marlins took Beckett with the second pick in the draft out of Spring High School, just north of Houston. They gave him a big contract and a bigger future. Scouts had been comparing him to Roger Clemens since he was 16, and Beckett didn’t argue.

Then the players went on strike, and the Marlins did what the Marlins do, which is nothing, slowly. While half of baseball was signing stars and starting new leagues, Florida sat on its hands and waited for the old MLB to come back. It never did.

Beckett spent most of the chaos pitching in whatever corner of the Marlins’ half-functioning system would have him. Last year he went 14-1 with 203 strikeouts in 139 minor league innings. Then he made four starts for Miami’s makeshift big league club in September and gave up four earned runs in 24 innings.

“I was ready,” Beckett says. “They weren’t. Nobody down there knew what league we were in. I’m not sure they do now.”

When the new league held its protection draft in January, Miami was holding on to veterans and a lot of John Henry’s money. Beckett had made it known, loudly, that he wanted to pitch in Texas. The Marlins, worn out or careless or both, left him off their protected list. The lower divisions picked first. San Antonio picked him.

“Best phone call of my life,” Beckett says. “Worst one of the Marlins’.”

Why here

The obvious Texas destination was Houston, 20 minutes from his parents’ house. Houston didn’t want him, or at least didn’t want him enough. The Astros had spent the strike buying Sammy Sosa and Alex Rodriguez, and a 21-year-old with four big league starts wasn’t on their shopping list.

San Antonio wanted him very much.

The Rebeldes were founded during the strike by a local businessman who wanted a team that celebrated both halves of what San Antonio is: Mexican and American, tacos and brisket, Tejano and country. Mostly it worked. The Rebeldes thrived in the Southwest league. The crowds are loud, bilingual and very serious about their nachos. There’s a mariachi band behind the bullpen.

The owner, Kyle Clayton, whose mother’s family came from Monterrey, flew to Spring the day after the draft and sat in Beckett’s parents’ kitchen.

“He brought a cooler of brisket from a place in Lockhart,” Beckett says. “I want to be clear that it was not the brisket that convinced me. But it didn’t hurt.”

What convinced him was a promise. Clayton told him that if he was as good as everybody said, he’d pitch Opening Day. And Opening Day, it happened, was at Monterrey.

The derby

You have to understand what San Antonio-Monterrey means down here.

The two cities are about 300 miles apart, on either side of a border that people in this part of the world cross for groceries, weddings and funerals. A lot of Rebeldes fans have family in Monterrey, and a lot of Sultanes fans have cousins in San Antonio. Their ballclubs have spent two years claiming the same people.

Monterrey also has Jeff Bagwell and Craig Biggio, the two greatest Astros ever, a fact Beckett has thought about more than he admits.

“I grew up watching those guys,” he says. “Biggio, Bagwell, the Killer B’s. I had a Biggio poster. Now I get to strike him out.”

He pauses.

“Or try. Try to strike him out. Don’t write that I said I would.”

Too late.

El Güero

The fans have already given Beckett a nickname: El Güero. It roughly means “the blond guy,” and it’s said with a mix of affection and amusement, the way you’d talk about a kid cousin who showed up to the family reunion in cowboy boots.

He loves it. He’s been taking Spanish lessons from the bullpen catcher, mostly so he can understand what the fans are yelling. “Half of it’s nice,” he says. “The other half’s about my hair.”

He’s also become, in a short time, the face of a team that didn’t have one. The Rebeldes are a young club: Justin Morneau, a 20-year-old first baseman from British Columbia, and Jayson Werth, a 22-year-old outfielder, are the future, and Julio Lugo plays shortstop. The rotation’s other half is Mark Buehrle, a soft-tossing lefty who was drafted in the 38th round and pitches like a man trying to catch a bus.

One preseason ranking put the Rebeldes second in the FBL and called Beckett and Buehrle “a strong FBL rotation foundation.” That’s high praise for a club that has never played in a league with more than six teams.

Beckett has his own projection.

“We’re going up,” he says. “This year. Then next year. Then I’m pitching in Houston. In front of everybody I went to high school with. On the visiting team.”

He grins.

“That’s the plan. Somebody write it down.”

One more thing

There’s a pitcher in this league Beckett has been compared to his whole life, and he’s here too: Roger Clemens, 39, now pitching for Louisville in the FBL after the most famous clubhouse fight of the century. Beckett hasn’t faced him yet. He has, however, already circled the date.

“Everybody’s said I’m the next Clemens since I was 16,” Beckett says. “I’d like to see if it’s true. You only find out one way.”

How?

“Beat him.”

He laces up his boots, puts on the camo hat and walks out toward the bullpen, where the mariachis are tuning up. One of the trumpet players yells something in Spanish, and Beckett yells something back, and the whole bullpen laughs.

I asked him later what he’d said.

“I told him to play louder on Monday,” Beckett said. “I want ‘em to hear it in Monterrey.”
