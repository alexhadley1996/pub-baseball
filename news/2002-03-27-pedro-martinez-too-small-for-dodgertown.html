MLB · 2002-03-27 · By Hal Brennan, Diamond Daily

Too Small for Dodgertown

Fourteen years after the Dodgers signed him and eight after they gave him away, Pedro Martinez is back in Vero Beach, and he didn't come alone.

VERO BEACH, Fla. — The best pitcher in the world is on a back field at Dodgertown, the one with the chain-link fence and the leaning palms, playing catch with the only man alive who’s allowed to interrupt him.

“Tek, the changeup, I think the grip—”

“Grip’s fine. You’re early with the arm.”

Pedro Martinez throws another one. It dies at Jason Varitek’s shins as if it had been shot.

“OK,” Pedro says, grinning. “Now the grip is fine.”

They’ve been doing this for the better part of four years, first in Fort Myers and Fenway, now here, 2,500 miles from Boston, in the uniform that was supposed to be Pedro’s all along. The Dodgers signed him out of Manoguayabo in 1988, when he was 16 and weighed about as much as a duffel bag of bats. He threw on these same fields. Eight years ago they decided he was too small to hold up as a starter and traded him away.

Now he’s back, and the first thing he did when he walked into the clubhouse was check the height chart by the trainer’s room.

“Five-eleven,” he said. “Same as when they sent me away. I’m still small. But now they need me.”

The one that got away

Every Dodgers fan over 25 knows the trade by heart. In November 1993, Los Angeles sent Pedro to Montreal for Delino DeShields, a good young second baseman who would never be anything close to what Pedro became. Within four years Pedro had won a Cy Young with the Expos. Then Montreal sent him to Boston for Carl Pavano and Tony Armas Jr., and he won two more, in 1999 and 2000. Some scouts still call that two-year stretch the best pitching they’ve ever seen.

DeShields, for the record, will open this season playing second base for the Austin Violets in the ABL. Pavano is in Richmond. Armas is in Colorado. Pedro is here.

“I don’t hate anybody for that trade,” Pedro says, and you believe him, mostly. “Tommy, Fred, everybody, they saw a skinny kid and they thought, this kid breaks. They were wrong, but they weren’t stupid. My brother was the big one. Ramon was the Dodger. I was Ramon’s little brother.”

Ramon Martinez won 20 games for the Dodgers in 1990 and threw a no-hitter for them in 1995. He’s here this spring, visiting, sitting in the stands behind the back field with his arms folded, watching his little brother play catch.

“He is still the little brother,” Ramon says. “He just has more trophies now.”

Leaving Boston

The question everyone in New England has been asking since January is simple: how did it come to this? How does the most beloved athlete in Boston, a man who once struck out five of the six hitters he faced in an All-Star Game at Fenway, end up in Los Angeles?

The answer, like everything else in this strange new world, starts with the strike. While other clubs broke ranks, formed regional leagues and spent like drunken sailors, the Red Sox spent two years insisting they were still the only true major league team left standing. Their stubbornness earned them a guaranteed place in the top tier. It also cost them almost everyone.

Manny Ramirez is in Milwaukee. Nomar Garciaparra is in Pittsburgh. Derek Lowe is in Montreal. Trot Nixon is in Santo Domingo. For Pedro, there was also the shoulder. He threw only 116.2 innings last year, with rotator cuff inflammation shutting him down for most of the second half. He still struck out 163 with a 2.39 ERA, but Boston started treating him like a museum piece.

“Boston was waiting for 1999 to come back,” Pedro says. “I love Boston. I will always love Boston. But I’m 30. I cannot wait for a year that is already gone.”

The Red Sox replaced him with Randy Johnson, which is about as close to replacing him as anyone could have done. Still, a longtime Fenway employee, who asked not to be named, put it plainly: “We got a great pitcher. LA got our pitcher.”

People in Boston have also noticed, loudly, that the Dodgers belong to News Corporation, whose chairman spent the winter drawing up the television deal that made this league possible. “The man who wrote the TV contract owns the team that got the best pitcher in baseball,” the Fenway employee said. “You do the math.”

The Dodgers, for their part, say Pedro called them.

“I come, he comes”

Pedro had one condition.

“I told them, ‘I come, he comes,’” he says, nodding toward Varitek, who is packing a three-ring binder into a duffel bag. “That was the whole negotiation. Five minutes.”

It’s fitting, because the two of them arrived in Boston the same way: as the punch line of somebody else’s bad trade. In July 1997, Seattle sent Varitek, its former first-round pick out of Georgia Tech, and a young pitcher named Derek Lowe to Boston for reliever Heathcliff Slocumb. Mariners fans still can’t talk about it. Four months later Pedro came from Montreal. Two lopsided trades, a summer and a fall apart, built the best battery in baseball.

Varitek doesn’t talk much, and when he does, it’s mostly about work. He has binders on every hitter in the NL West: Sosa and A-Rod in Houston, Helton in Chicago, Pujols in Oakland. Pedro has never faced most of them. Varitek has studied all of them anyway.

There’s one hitter he doesn’t need a page for.

“Manny,” Varitek says. “I watched him hit every day last year. I don’t need a binder.”

What happens when Pedro faces Manny for the first time?

Varitek almost smiles. “We’ll have a plan.”

Pedro does not almost smile. He laughs so loudly that a groundskeeper on the next field turns around.

“Manny is going to swing at the first pitch,” he says. “Manny always swings at the first pitch when it’s me. In batting practice, in the clubhouse, at dinner. He’s going to swing, and I’m going to know.”

Asked what makes the partnership work, Varitek takes a long time to answer. “My job is to know what he wants to throw before he does,” he says finally. “Some days that’s easy. Some days he changes his mind on purpose, just to see if I’m paying attention.”

The thin blue line

Here’s the uncomfortable part. The Dodgers aren’t very good.

Juan Gonzalez came over from the wreckage in Cleveland, and David Segui can still hit. After that, the lineup thins out fast, with rookies Shane Victorino and Wilson Betemit expected to play every day. Behind Pedro, the rotation is Jose Lima and Dave Mlicki and a lot of hope. The preseason consensus has Los Angeles near the bottom of the West, and under this league’s rules, the last-place team in each division goes down to the ABL.

The Los Angeles Dodgers. Relegated. Say it out loud and it still sounds like a typo.

“If Pedro makes 32 starts, they’re a .500 team,” one NL scout said. “If he makes 20, they’re in the ABL next year, and he’s the most expensive pitcher in the second division.”

Pedro waves it off. “Relegation,” he says, sounding out the word. “I don’t know this word. Teach me.”

“He knows it,” Varitek says, without looking up.

Manager Jim Tracy has already named Pedro the starter for Sunday’s opener, which falls on Easter. Pedro, a man of real faith, likes that. Tracy likes something else.

“Everybody knows what he does on the mound,” Tracy says. “What I didn’t expect is how much he cares about the 20-year-olds. He’s out here at 7:30 with kids who were in middle school when he won his first Cy Young.”

One of those kids is Adam Wainwright, a 6-foot-7 right-hander who turned 20 in the offseason and has been following Pedro around camp like a very tall shadow.

“Look at him,” Pedro says, pointing. “Six-seven. When I was his age, the Dodgers told me I was too small. Now they have him, and they say he’s too young. They are never happy here.”

Then he walks over and spends 20 minutes showing Wainwright the changeup grip.

Home

At the end of the morning, the two of them walk back toward the clubhouse past the Dodgertown fields where Pedro threw as a teenager. He stops for a second at one of them.

“In Manoguayabo there was a mango tree,” he says. “I sat under it as a boy and I dreamed about Dodger Stadium, because that’s where Ramon was. Then I got there, and they sent me away, and I dreamed about other places. Montreal. Boston. Now I’m back here.”

He shrugs.

“I’m not dreaming anymore. Now I’m just working.”

Varitek is 10 feet ahead of him, binder under his arm, already thinking about Sunday. Pedro jogs to catch up, says something in his ear, and for the first time all morning, Varitek laughs.

Nobody else heard the joke. That’s sort of the point.
